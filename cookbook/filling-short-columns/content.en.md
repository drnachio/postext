# The Lens That Lit the Coast {kicker="History · Lights" standfirst="Two hundred years ago a road engineer in poor health took the mirrors out of a lighthouse and put rings of glass in their place. His lenses still turn on a few headlands, fewer every winter." byline="Words by Marta Quiroga" credit="Painting made for this issue"}

The tower of Cordouan stands on a reef at the mouth of the Gironde, six kilometres from either shore, and at high tide it seems to stand on the sea itself. The masons of Louis de Foix began it in 1584 and finished it in 1611, a lighthouse dressed as a palace, with a royal chapel on its third floor. For two centuries its light was a fire of oak and pitch, and later of whale oil, burning in an open brazier under the lantern. Sailors coming in from the Bay of Biscay often saw the tower before they saw its fire. On a hazy night they saw neither, and more than one ship found the reef of Cordouan before it found the channel into the river and the port of Bordeaux beyond it.

In the summer of 1823 a new apparatus went up the stairs of Cordouan in crates. It was a cage of brass, taller than a man, holding panels of glass cut into steps, and in the middle of it a single oil lamp with several concentric wicks. On the first night it was lit, the light reached the pilots at Royan more clearly than the old reflectors ever had. The engineer who designed it, Augustin Fresnel, was thirty-five and already ill with the tuberculosis that would kill him four years later.

Fresnel was not a lighthouse man. He trained at the École polytechnique and spent his first years building roads in the Drôme for the Corps des Ponts et Chaussées, and he did his real work at night, on the nature of light. His memoir on diffraction won the prize of the Academy of Sciences in 1819, against judges who expected light to be a stream of particles. That same year the Commission des Phares, the body in charge of France's coastal lights, asked him to look at its lamps. He found a waste of light that offended him as a physicist.

The lamp came first. With the physicist François Arago, Fresnel built a burner with several round wicks set one inside another, fed with oil from below by a small pump so that the flames never ran dry. It gave a broad, steady flame, much brighter than one wick, but a brighter flame was no use if most of it lit the inside of the lantern. The question was how to send it out to sea.

## A lamp in a ring of prisms

Before Fresnel, the best lighthouses used reflectors. An Argand lamp burned at the focus of a polished copper bowl, silvered on the inside, and the bowl threw the light forward in a beam. A tower needed a dozen or more of these lamps and mirrors, each with its own wick to trim. The silver tarnished, the bowls were never quite parabolic, and much of the light that fell on them was absorbed or scattered. Keepers spent their days polishing, and the beam was still weak.

A lens could do better, in theory, but a lens large enough for a lighthouse would have been a block of glass thick at the centre, heavy enough to crack its own frame, and too cloudy to pass much light. Buffon had suggested a century earlier that a burning glass could be cut into steps, keeping only the curved surface that does the bending. Fresnel took the idea further. He built his lens from separate rings, each ground to its own curve and all bringing the light to the same point, so that no piece of glass was thicker than a few centimetres.

Around the central belt of rings he added prisms above and below. These catch the light that a lens would let escape towards the sky or the sea and turn it back towards the horizon, by refraction and then, in the outer rows, by reflection inside the glass itself. Fresnel worked out these reflecting prisms in the last years of his life, writing to his brother Léonor about their angles. He did not live to see a light built with them from top to bottom: Léonor, who took over his post at the Commission, saw the first ones lit in the 1840s.

The result was a lamp in a ring of prisms that sent most of its light where a ship could see it. A single wick in a large Fresnel lens did the work of the twenty or so lamps and mirrors it replaced, on a fraction of the oil. On a clear night the light of a coast station could be seen from more than twenty nautical miles, limited less by the lamp than by the curve of the Earth.

The glass itself came in pieces. Each ring of a large lens was ground on a turning table against a cast-iron tool of the right curve, then polished with rouge for days, and the finished pieces were bedded in their brass frame with putty. The lens arrived at the lighthouse as a kit of several hundred parts, and the engineer who put it together had to align every ring with the flame to within a millimetre or two, by eye, at night, moving a lamp and watching where its light fell on the rocks below.

## Seven orders of glass

The Commission soon needed lenses of several sizes, and Fresnel's successors sorted them into orders by focal distance, the space between the flame and the inner face of the glass. A first-order lens, for the great coastal lights, had a focal distance of 920 millimetres and stood about two and a half metres high. A sixth-order lens, for a harbour pier or a river mouth, had 150 millimetres and could be carried by two people. Between them came the second, third and fourth orders, with a three-and-a-half added later for the middle distances of estuaries.

Each lens had to say which light it was. A sailor who sees a light on a dark coast needs to know whether it is Cordouan, the Pointe de Grave or a fishing village, and the answer is in its rhythm. Fixed lights burned steadily. Flashing lights used panels of rings like bull's eyes, set round the lamp and turned by clockwork, so that each panel swept a beam across the sea as it passed. The number of panels and the speed of the turn gave each tower its own count of flashes and dark intervals, printed in the lists of lights that every navigator carried.

The clockwork had to be wound by hand. A heavy weight hung in a tube running down the centre of the tower, and the keeper on watch cranked it back up every few hours through the night. If he fell asleep, the lens slowed and stopped, and the light lost its character: to a ship offshore it became another light, or no light at all. In the 1890s French engineers floated the rotating lens on a bath of mercury, so that a small push turned several tonnes of brass and glass. The lens could turn faster, and the flashes grew shorter and brighter.

Paris made most of the lenses. The workshops of Henry-Lepaute, Sautter and Barbier ground and polished the rings by hand and shipped them in numbered crates to coasts all over the world, with drawings for assembling them on the spot. Chance Brothers, near Birmingham, made glass for the British lights and later lenses of their own, including the largest of all, the hyperradiant lenses of the 1880s, with a focal distance of 1,330 millimetres. In the United States, the Lighthouse Board set up in 1852 replaced the old reflectors with French lenses within a decade.

## When the keepers left

A lens could last for more than a century. The glass did not wear out, and the brass needed only cleaning. What changed around the lenses was the lamp. Oil gave way to paraffin vapour burners with incandescent mantles at the end of the nineteenth century, then to electric lamps, at first in the towers that had a supply nearby and later almost everywhere. The light grew brighter, but the lens around it stayed the same, because it already put nearly all of that light on the horizon.

For the keepers, the routine hardly changed in a hundred years. At sunset they lit the lamp and wound the clockwork; through the night they took turns on watch in the service room under the lantern, writing the weather and the state of the light in the station's log every few hours; at dawn they put the light out, drew linen curtains round the lens so that the sun would not set fire to anything through it, and began cleaning. Brass was polished, glass wiped with spirit and a soft cloth, never anything harder.

Electricity also made the keepers unnecessary. A lamp that did not need its wick trimmed and a motor that did not need winding could be watched from a distance. Through the second half of the twentieth century, lighthouse after lighthouse was automated, its family moved ashore and its door locked. The last keepers of the British lights left North Foreland, in Kent, in November 1998. In France the keepers of the offshore towers, the ones the service called hells, were among the last to go.

The lenses went next. A modern beacon of light-emitting diodes weighs a few kilograms, uses less power than a kettle and needs no mercury, which has its own risks for those who climb the tower. Some lenses were taken down and sold, some broke in storage, and some were cut into pieces for collectors. The ones that survive have mostly gone to museums, where they are lit by a bulb inside and look like what they are, a machine for sending light a long way.

A few still work. At Cordouan a first-order lens still turns in the lantern, and the reef that holds the tower became a World Heritage site in 2021. Visitors who make the crossing at low tide can climb the stairs past the chapel to the lantern gallery and stand beside the rings of glass. The light still shows its own rhythm to the pilots of the Gironde, white over the channel and coloured over the shoals, as the ships coming in from Biscay have read it for two hundred years.
