# Science as a Social Practice {kicker="HPS 214 · Seminar reading list · Autumn term 2026" lead="Six weeks on how scientific knowledge is made, tested and trusted, from the paradigm to the laboratory notebook."}

This seminar reads the history and sociology of science as one subject. Its question is how a claim about nature becomes a fact that people who never saw the experiment are willing to rely on. The answers we read come from historians of the seventeenth century, from sociologists who sat in laboratories for two years, and from philosophers who looked at what scientists actually do rather than at what their textbooks say they do.

Each week has one set reading, which everyone reads before the seminar, and one or two companion texts for the person introducing the discussion. The set readings are available in the course reader; the others are in the library’s reserve collection.

## Week 1 · Paradigms and normal science

We start with the book that made the subject fashionable. Read the first five sections and the Postscript of @kuhn_structure_1970, which Kuhn added for the second edition to answer the charge that “paradigm” meant too many things at once. His preface credits a monograph by Ludwik Fleck, written in 1935 and nearly forgotten until Kuhn mentioned it; it is in the list at the end, and the student introducing the week should read its second chapter.

## Week 2 · Witnessing an experiment

The set reading is the first two chapters of @shapin_leviathan_1985, on Robert Boyle’s air-pump and the dispute with Thomas Hobbes over whether an experiment could settle anything. The companion text is the shorter article in which Shapin first described Boyle’s “literary technology”, the way a report written in plain, circumstantial prose turned its readers into witnesses [@shapin_pump_1984].

## Week 3 · Inside the laboratory

Bruno Latour spent two years at the Salk Institute as an observer of a neuroendocrinology laboratory. Read the second and third chapters of @latour_laboratory_1979 and follow how a statement loses its qualifications (“it seems that”, “X has claimed”) as it travels from one paper to the next until it is printed without any.

## Week 4 · Where science ends

The demarcation problem asks what separates science from what is not science. Philosophers looked for a criterion; @gieryn_boundary-work_1983 looked at the people who draw the line in practice, and at what they gain by drawing it where they do. Bring an example from the news of the last month.

## Week 5 · Objectivity has a history

Read the article first [@daston_image_1992], then the first chapter and the chapter on “trained judgment” in the book it grew into [@daston_objectivity_2007]. The argument is that objectivity, as a virtue of the scientist, appeared in the nineteenth century, and that atlases of skeletons, snowflakes and clouds show it arriving.

## Week 6 · Whom to believe

The last week returns to the seventeenth century with a broader question: how did gentlemen-philosophers decide whose reports to accept? Read the first chapter of @shapin_social_1994 and come ready to compare its answer with how you decide whom to believe when you read a paper outside your field.

## Further reading

The list below is the seminar’s Zotero collection, exported as BibTeX and set as it came out of Zotero. It includes every work cited above and five that the seminar does not discuss but that you will meet in the essays you read: Fleck’s monograph, Merton’s collected essays on the norms of science, Latour’s later handbook, Rheinberger’s study of experimental systems, and a history of Spanish science in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries for those who read Spanish.
