## A workshop in Mainz

Around :mark[1450]{style="date"} a goldsmith named :mark[Johannes Gutenberg]{style="name"} was running a printing workshop in :mark[Mainz]{style="name"}, a city on the Rhine. Printing itself was not new. Printers in China had cut whole pages into wooden blocks for centuries, and Korean printers had cast metal type by the :mark[thirteenth century]{style="date"}. What Gutenberg put together was a system that suited the twenty-odd letters of the Latin alphabet.

The system had four parts. The first was :mark[movable type]{style="term"}: single letters cast in metal, set in lines, printed and then put back in the case for the next page. A hand mould cast thousands of identical letters from an alloy of lead, tin and antimony. An ink made with linseed oil clung to the metal where a watery ink would have run off. And a :mark[screw press]{style="term"}, of the kind used to press grapes and to flatten wet paper, pushed the paper evenly onto the inked type.

The first large book from the workshop was a Latin Bible in two volumes, set in two columns of 42 lines, finished by :mark[1455]{style="date"}. About 180 copies were printed, most on paper and some on vellum. A single scribe could spend years copying one Bible; the workshop produced its whole edition in about the same time.

Gutenberg had borrowed heavily to build all this. His lender, the Mainz merchant :mark[Johann Fust]{style="name"}, took him to court in :mark[1455]{style="date"} and kept much of the equipment. Fust carried on the business with :mark[Peter Schöffer]{style="name"}, who had trained in the workshop. Their Psalter of :mark[1457]{style="date"} is the first printed book whose :mark[colophon]{style="term"}, the closing note of a book, names its printers and the day it was finished.

## The craft travels

For about ten years printing stayed close to Mainz. In :mark[1462]{style="date"} the city was sacked in a war between two rival archbishops, and many of its craftsmen left. Printers trained on the Rhine set up wherever they found readers, paper and money: at :mark[Subiaco]{style="name"}, near Rome, in :mark[1465]{style="date"}; in :mark[Venice]{style="name"} in :mark[1469]{style="date"}; in :mark[Paris]{style="name"}, in rooms at the Sorbonne, in :mark[1470]{style="date"}. A press was at work in :mark[Segovia]{style="name"} around :mark[1472]{style="date"}, and :mark[Valencia]{style="name"} had one by :mark[1474]{style="date"}.

:mark[William Caxton]{style="name"}, an English merchant who had learned the craft in :mark[Cologne]{style="name"}, set up his press at :mark[Westminster]{style="name"} in :mark[1476]{style="date"}. Most printers of the time worked in Latin, the language of the Church and the universities, and sold across borders. Caxton printed mostly in the :mark[vernacular]{style="term"}, the language people spoke at home: romances, histories and Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*, for readers who had no Latin.

Books printed before :mark[1501]{style="date"} are called :mark[incunabula]{style="term"}, from the Latin word for a cradle: the books of printing's infancy. Catalogues today list about 30,000 editions from those fifty years. By :mark[1500]{style="date"} presses had worked in more than 250 towns, and estimates of the copies they printed run from eight to twenty million.

## Venice and the pocket book

By the end of the century :mark[Venice]{style="name"} had more printing houses than any other city in Europe. It had paper mills on the mainland, ships to carry books to every port of the Mediterranean, and buyers with money. :mark[Aldus Manutius]{style="name"} opened his press there in the :mark[mid-1490s]{style="date"} and printed the Greek classics in editions prepared by scholars. His books carried a printer's mark, a dolphin wrapped round an anchor, so that buyers could tell them from the copies other printers soon made.

In :mark[1501]{style="date"} Aldus issued a Virgil in :mark[octavo]{style="term"}, a sheet folded three times to make sixteen pages, so that the book fitted in a hand. It was set in a slanting letter cut by :mark[Francesco Griffo]{style="name"}, which we now call :mark[italic]{style="term"}. The narrow letters fitted more words on each line, so the small books needed less paper and cost less. Readers could carry a poet in a pocket instead of reading him at a desk.

## Print and the Reformation

In :mark[1517]{style="date"} :mark[Martin Luther]{style="name"}, who taught theology at :mark[Wittenberg]{style="name"}, wrote ninety-five theses against the sale of indulgences. Printers in several German cities reprinted them within weeks. Luther went on to write short :mark[pamphlets]{style="term"} in German: a few pages each, quick to set, cheap to buy and easy to pass from hand to hand. Historians estimate that between :mark[1518]{style="date"} and :mark[1525]{style="date"} his writings made up about a third of all the books printed in German.

His opponents answered in print too, and the quarrel over the Church was argued in thousands of pamphlets read aloud in taverns and market squares. Printing did not cause the Reformation, but it carried each side's arguments further and faster than any preacher could.

## Glossary

:::paragraphs{style="glossary"}
**:mark[colophon]{style="term"}** A note at the end of a book that gives the printer, the place and the date of printing.

**:mark[incunabula]{style="term"}** Books printed in Europe before 1501. One such book is an incunable.

**:mark[italic]{style="term"}** A slanting style of type, first cut by Francesco Griffo for Aldus Manutius.

**:mark[movable type]{style="term"}** Single letters cast one by one, which can be set in lines, printed and used again.

**:mark[octavo]{style="term"}** A book whose sheets are folded three times, giving sixteen pages to the sheet.

**:mark[pamphlet]{style="term"}** A short booklet of a few unbound pages, quick and cheap to print.

**:mark[screw press]{style="term"}** A press in which a turning screw drives a flat plate down onto the paper and the type.

**:mark[vernacular]{style="term"}** The language people speak at home, as opposed to Latin.
:::
