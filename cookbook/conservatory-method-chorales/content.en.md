# Cadences {kicker="Chapter 4" deck="Where a phrase comes to rest, and how the four voices take it there"}

A phrase of music breathes out at its end, and the chord that closes it tells the listener how far the music has come to rest. That closing formula is the cadence. Students who can already connect any two chords in four parts tend to treat the last two chords of a phrase like any others, and their exercises end without conviction. This chapter slows down at the end of the phrase. It names the five cadences of the major key, shows how each one is voiced, and follows them through two chorale melodies that every harmony class sings: *Nun danket alle Gott* and the Old Hundredth.

:ref{id="ex-cadences" style="full"} sets the five side by side in G major, one to a bar, with the Roman numerals under the bass. Play them at the keyboard before you read on, and listen for how final each one sounds: the order of the bars runs from the most closed to the most open.

::resource{id="ex-cadences"}

## The authentic cadence

An authentic cadence is the progression V–I. It is the strongest close the key has, because the bass falls a fifth (or rises a fourth) from the dominant to the tonic while the leading tone, the seventh degree, rises a semitone to the tonic in one of the upper voices.

We call it **perfect** when both chords are in root position and the soprano ends on the tonic. If either condition fails, the cadence is **imperfect**: the soprano ends on the third or the fifth of the tonic chord, or one of the two chords is inverted. The first two bars of :ref{id="ex-cadences" case="lower"} keep the same bass and change the top: F sharp rising to G in the first, A rising to B in the second.

The leading tone needs care. In the soprano it must rise to the tonic. In an inner voice it may fall a third to the fifth of the tonic chord, so that the final chord is complete, and Bach does this in many of his chorales; but never let it fall in the bass or the soprano. After a dominant seventh the seventh itself (C in G major) resolves down by step. If the leading tone rises as well, the tonic chord that follows has a tripled root and no fifth; letting it fall in an inner voice keeps the chord complete. The last bar of :ref{id="ex-nun-danket" case="lower"} takes the second way: the tenor holds C from the chord before and falls to B, the alto lets F sharp drop to D, and the soprano sings A–G over the bass D–G.

::resource{id="ex-nun-danket"}

## The plagal cadence

The progression IV–I is the plagal cadence. It has no leading tone, and its effect is gentler; hymn books use it for the *Amen* after a final authentic cadence. In four parts the tonic is the common tone of the two chords and stays in the same voice, while the other two upper voices move down by step, the sixth degree to the fifth and the fourth to the third, as in the fourth bar of :ref{id="ex-cadences" case="lower"}.

The first line of *Nun danket alle Gott* ends this way in our setting. The soprano repeats E over C and then falls to D over G (bars 1–2 of :ref{id="ex-nun-danket" case="lower"}). The cadence comes on the downbeat and the fermata is short, so the singers take a breath and go on: a plagal close in the middle of a hymn reads as a comma, not a full stop.

## The half cadence

A phrase that stops on the dominant ends with a **half cadence**. Any chord may lead to it, most often I, IV or ii, and the dominant is always in root position. The ear hears a question waiting for its answer, which is why the half cadence closes the first half of so many tunes and almost never the last line. In the third line of *Nun danket alle Gott* (:ref{id="ex-nun-danket-3" case="lower"}) the tonic in first inversion leads to V under a soprano that falls from B to A.

::resource{id="ex-nun-danket-3"}

Many half cadences approach the dominant through the cadential six-four: the tonic chord with its fifth in the bass, on a strong beat, followed by the dominant on the same bass note. The sixth and the fourth above the bass fall by step to the fifth and the third. Treat the six-four as an ornament of the dominant, not as a tonic, and double its bass.

## Four cadences in one tune

The Old Hundredth, printed with Psalm 134 in the Genevan Psalter of 1551, closes its four lines on three kinds of cadence. :ref{id="ex-old-hundredth" style="full"} sets it complete, barred in 2/2, with the long and short notes of the Genevan rhythm.

::resource{id="ex-old-hundredth"}

- **Line 1** ends on B over G: an imperfect authentic cadence, because the soprano stops on the third.
- **Line 2** ends with the cadential six-four and V, a half cadence with the suspension in the soprano.
- **Line 3** ends F sharp–G over D–G: a perfect authentic cadence with the leading tone resolving in the soprano. Coming before the last line, it is answered by a stronger close.
- **Line 4** descends B–A–G over the cadential six-four, V and I: the most complete close of the four.

Sing the bass while a classmate plays the upper voices: the bass line alone tells you which cadence is coming.

## The deceptive cadence

When V moves to vi instead of I, the cadence is **deceptive**. The leading tone still rises to the tonic, but the bass climbs a step to the sixth degree, and the phrase that seemed finished has to go on. Double the third of vi (the tonic of the key) so that the leading tone can resolve and the other voices fall without parallel octaves.

:::callout{type="rule" title="Before you write the cadence"}
- Decide the cadence from the melody first: the last two notes of the soprano allow only some of them.
- Put the dominant in root position for authentic, half and deceptive cadences.
- Resolve the leading tone up in the outer voices, and the seventh of V^7^ down.
- Check the outer voices for parallel and hidden fifths and octaves.
:::

Composers use the deceptive cadence to lengthen a phrase. :ref{id="ex-deceptive" style="full"} sings the last line of the Old Hundredth twice: the first time it closes on vi, an E minor chord under the tonic note, and the second time on the perfect cadence of :ref{id="ex-old-hundredth" case="lower"}.

::resource{id="ex-deceptive"}

## In the minor key

Everything in this chapter holds in minor, with one condition: the leading tone is not in the key signature. In A minor the dominant chord is E–G sharp–B, and the sharp has to be written each time; without it the chord is minor and the authentic cadence loses its pull. In the deceptive cadence the raised leading tone rises to the tonic and the sixth degree, F, sits a semitone above the dominant in the bass, so the alto or the tenor must avoid the augmented second G sharp–F.

Two cadences belong to the minor mode. A chorale in minor often closes on a major tonic chord, raising its third at the last moment: this is the *tierce de Picardie*, which Bach uses at the end of most of his minor chorales. And a half cadence may approach the dominant from the first inversion of iv, with the bass falling a semitone from the sixth degree to the fifth: the Phrygian half cadence, common at the end of a slow movement before a fast one.

## Exercises

1. Write the five cadences of :ref{id="ex-cadences" case="lower"} in D major and in F major, in close and in open position.
2. Harmonize the first line of the Old Hundredth with a perfect authentic cadence in place of the imperfect one. Which note of the melody must change?
3. In three chorales of your choice, find the cadence at the end of each line, label it and circle the leading tone where it resolves.
4. Rewrite the last two bars of :ref{id="ex-old-hundredth" case="lower"} with a plagal *Amen* after the final chord, keeping the tonic in the same voice.
