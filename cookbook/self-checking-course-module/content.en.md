# Earthquakes {kicker="Module 4 · The Restless Planet" lead="Why the ground shakes, how the shaking travels and how we measure it."}

Most of the time the rocks under our feet seem perfectly still. They are not. The plates of the Earth's outer shell creep past one another at about the speed your fingernails grow, a few centimetres a year. Where two plates meet, the rocks on either side of a fault are often locked together by friction, so the movement does not happen smoothly. Instead the rocks bend, storing energy the way a ruler stores it when you bend it over the edge of a desk.

## Where the ground breaks

An earthquake begins when the stress on a locked fault grows larger than the friction holding it. The fault slips, often by a few metres in a few seconds, and the bent rocks on each side spring back towards their old shape. This is the elastic rebound theory, put forward by the geologist Harry Fielding Reid after the San Francisco earthquake of 1906, when fences and roads that crossed the San Andreas Fault were found offset by as much as six metres.

The point underground where the slip starts is the **hypocentre**, or focus. The point on the surface directly above it is the **epicentre**, the place news reports name. Most earthquakes happen along plate boundaries, and most of the largest ones around the rim of the Pacific Ocean, where ocean plates sink beneath their neighbours.

## Waves through the Earth

The energy released at the focus spreads out as seismic waves. The first to arrive are **P waves** (primary waves), which squeeze and stretch the rock in the direction they travel, like sound. They pass through solids and liquids at about 6 km per second in the crust. Next come **S waves** (secondary waves), which shake the rock from side to side. They are slower, about 3.5 km per second, and they cannot pass through liquids. Last come the surface waves, which roll along the ground and do most of the damage.

Because the two kinds of body wave travel at different speeds, the gap between them grows with distance. A seismograph that records the P wave and then, 20 seconds later, the S wave is about 160 km from the epicentre: roughly 8 km for every second of delay. One station gives the distance; three stations, each drawing a circle of its own distance on a map, give the epicentre where the circles cross.

## Measuring a quake

Seismologists describe an earthquake with two different numbers. Its **magnitude** measures the size of the event at its source, and an earthquake has one magnitude only. Each whole step on the scale releases about 32 times more energy, so a magnitude 7 releases about a thousand times more than a magnitude 5. The largest earthquake ever recorded struck Valdivia, in southern Chile, on 22 May 1960, at magnitude 9.5.

**Intensity** measures the shaking at one place, from what people felt and what was damaged. It is given in Roman numerals on the Modified Mercalli scale, from I (felt by almost no one) to XII (damage almost total), and one earthquake has many intensities, falling away with distance from the epicentre.
