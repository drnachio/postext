# What Fixity Meant {author="Daniel Okafor" short="What Fixity Meant"}

Few books in the history of the book have been argued with as long as Elizabeth Eisenstein’s. *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* appeared in two volumes in 1979 and proposed that the shift from script to print was itself a cause of the Renaissance revival of learning, of the Reformation and of the new science of the seventeenth century [@eisenstein1979]. Her case rested on a few features that print had and manuscript did not: dissemination in hundreds of copies at once, standardisation of text and image, and above all what she called typographical fixity, the capacity of an edition to preserve a text unchanged and to let it be corrected and improved in the next.

The thesis was large, and its reception divided along a familiar line. Historians of ideas found in it an explanation of why scholarship became cumulative after 1500. Historians of printing pointed to the variety of what presses actually produced, from error-filled almanacs to pirated editions, and asked whether fixity was a property of print or a result that some printers achieved some of the time.

## Credit and piracy

Adrian Johns gave the second answer its fullest form [@johns1998]. Studying the London book trade of the seventeenth century, he found that readers had good reason to distrust what they bought. Editions were reprinted without permission, attributed to authors who had not written them, or issued with the wrong date and imprint. The Stationers’ Company kept a register of titles to settle disputes among its members, but the register protected the trade’s own arrangements rather than the author’s text. A reader who wanted to know whether a printed book was what it claimed to be had to rely on the reputation of the printer and the bookseller, on the people who vouched for the edition, and on the conventions of the shop where it was sold.

Fixity, in Johns’s account, was therefore something made. It took the work of authors who watched over their proofs, of booksellers who guarded their names, and of institutions such as the Royal Society, which licensed and supervised the books it sponsored. His most cited example is the Astronomer Royal, John Flamsteed, who saw his observations printed in 1712 in an edition he had not approved and spent years trying to recover and suppress it. Where Eisenstein saw a feature of the technology, Johns saw a set of practices that had to be learned, and that could fail.

## Circuits and models

The debate between the two positions is easier to follow with a map of what a book passes through. Robert Darnton drew one in 1982: a communications circuit that runs from the author to the publisher, the printers, the shippers, the booksellers and the readers, and back to the author, with each stage shaped by economic, political and intellectual conditions [@darnton1982]. The model placed the history of books among the people who handled them, and many studies of the 1980s worked through it stage by stage.

Thomas Adams and Nicolas Barker objected that Darnton’s circuit described the trade and lost sight of the object. They proposed a model built around the book itself and the events in its life: publication, manufacture, distribution, reception and survival [@adams1993]. The difference matters to the argument about fixity. In Darnton’s circuit, fixity belongs to the relations between printers, sellers and readers; in Adams and Barker’s model, it is also a question of which copies survived, where, and in what state.

## The form of the text

D. F. McKenzie widened the question further in his Panizzi lectures, published in 1986 [@mckenzie1986]. Bibliography, he argued, should study texts as recorded forms and the processes of their transmission, including their production and their reception, and it should treat the form of a text as part of its meaning. His example was William Congreve, whose plays changed character when they were reset for the collected *Works* of 1710: the new layout divided the scenes differently, marked entrances in a new way and invited a different kind of reading.

From McKenzie’s position the quarrel over fixity looks less like a quarrel. An edition can fix the words of a text and still change how it reads; a reprint can keep the meaning and lose the form that carried it. Eisenstein described what the press made possible, and Johns described what it took to make that possibility hold [@eisenstein1979; @johns1998]. Both are histories of the same pages, written from different ends of the circuit.
