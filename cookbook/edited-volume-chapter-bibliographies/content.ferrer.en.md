# Punches, Matrices and the Name Garamond {author="Lucía Ferrer" short="Punches and Matrices"}

Before a compositor could set a page, someone had to make the letters he picked from the case. Printing spread quickly: Lucien Febvre and Henri-Jean Martin estimated that fifteen to twenty million copies of books had been printed in Europe before 1500 [@febvre1976]. Every one of those copies was printed from type cast in a mould, and every typeface began as a set of steel punches cut by hand at the size the reader would see.

The sequence of work is well documented, though the hand skills it required have mostly been lost [@carter1969]. The punchcutter shaped the end of a steel bar into the reversed form of a letter, filing and engraving it and checking it against smoke proofs. The enclosed spaces of the letter, the counters of *a*, *e* or *o*, were usually struck into the punch with a counterpunch, which let him repeat the same counter in several letters of the fount. Fred Smeijers, who has cut punches himself, argues that the counterpunch explains much of the consistency of sixteenth-century faces: the shapes were related because they were made with the same tools [@smeijers1996].

## From punch to sort

The hardened punch was driven into a bar of copper to make a matrix. The matrix had to be justified, filed so that the letter sat at the right depth and position, and then fitted into a hand mould, an adjustable box of steel and wood into which the caster poured an alloy of lead, tin and antimony. Each pour gave one sort, and the finished type, rubbed and dressed, was sold to printers by weight [@gaskell1972].

Punches were the founder’s capital. They could be used for decades, lent, inherited or sold, and a set of strikes from them allowed another foundry to cast the same face. This is why the faces of a few French punchcutters of the mid-sixteenth century spread across Europe within a generation.

## Whose letters

It also made attribution difficult. A face appears in a printer’s book long after it was cut, under the printer’s name and not the cutter’s. Harry Carter’s survey of early typography set out the evidence available to sort these questions: specimen sheets, inventories, contracts and the books themselves [@carter1969]. Hendrik Vervliet spent decades applying it to the French Renaissance, comparing faces letter by letter across hundreds of books, and his catalogues redistributed many types long credited to Claude Garamont among his contemporaries, Robert Granjon and Pierre Haultin among them [@vervliet2008].

The name Garamond had a modern history of its own. When foundries in the early twentieth century revived “Garamond” for machine composition, several of them took as their model types that had been cut not by Garamont but by Jean Jannon, a printer at Sedan, some sixty years after Garamont’s death. Beatrice Warde pointed this out in 1926. The revivals kept the name, and many digital faces still carry it.

## What the reader sees

None of this is visible on the page as a reader meets it. The reader judges a book by how easily it reads. The bibliographer sees, in the same page, the trace of a punchcutter’s files, the wear of a matrix and the habits of the caster who justified it. Early printers bought their type by size names, pica, english, long primer, and judged it as the reader did, by the page it made [@gaskell1972]. The history of type begins in steel, but it was always written for that page.
