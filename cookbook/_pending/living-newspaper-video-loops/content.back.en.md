# Stage & Screen {style="section"}

## A Lady of the Garden at the Pavilion {style="second"}

:::paragraphs{style="byline"}
Reviewed by **Harold Vosper**
:::

:::paragraphs{style="flush"}
The Pavilion Players opened their summer season on Monday with *A Lady of the Garden*, a comedy in three acts by a playwright who prefers to remain anonymous, and whose modesty the second act fully justifies. The first and third, however, are a delight, and the evening belongs to Miss Rosalind Avery.
:::

::resource{id="actress"}

As Miss Hester Lane, a gardener’s daughter who inherits the great house where her father kept the roses, Miss Avery is by turns wistful, imperious and very funny. Her long speech to the assembled relations in the third act, delivered from the top of the garden steps with one arm flung out towards the vegetable beds, stopped the play for a full minute of applause on the first night.

Mr Frank Polglase is a fine, glowering butler, who conveys more with one raised eyebrow than the second act manages with its whole cast, and Miss Daisy Kitto, as the cousin who expected to inherit, is spiteful in the best possible way. The painted garden by Mr Tom Rule, the company’s carpenter, is so convincing that a lady in the second row was heard to ask where she might buy the delphiniums.

The second act is another matter. It is set in a solicitor’s office, it lasts fifty minutes, and it consists almost entirely of people reading a will aloud to one another. Miss Avery does her best with a scene in which she must faint on to a filing cabinet, but the audience on Monday was plainly waiting for the garden to come back, and when it did, so did the play.

*A Lady of the Garden* runs nightly at eight until the 24th, with a matinée on Wednesdays at half past two. Seats from sixpence to three shillings; the box office at the Pavilion is open from ten.

## Band concert on the Parade {style="third"}

The Wexcombe Silver Band gives the first of its Sunday concerts on the Parade bandstand tomorrow at three, under its new conductor, Mr Alfred Penna, late of the Royal Marines. The programme includes a selection from *The Gondoliers*, the overture to *Poet and Peasant* and a march of Mr Penna’s own, *The Harbour Line*, written for the opening of the trams and played for the first time in public. Deck chairs twopence; the collection is for the hospital.

## Harbour paintings at the Institute {style="third"}

The Wexcombe Art Society’s summer exhibition opened at the Literary Institute on Wednesday, with a hundred and twelve pictures by forty-one members, the largest show in the society’s history. The harbour, as ever, is the favourite subject: there are nineteen views of it, at every hour and in every weather, and one, by Miss Phyllis Body, painted from the top of the Guildhall tower while the clockmakers were at work. The prize for the best picture in oils went to Mr Ernest Lugg for *Pilchard Boats, Evening*, and the prize for water-colours to Mrs Jane Treloar, whose *Quay Hill in Rain* shows the new tram lines glistening, two days before they were finished. The exhibition is open daily from ten until six, until the 1st of July. Admission threepence; members free.

# Sport {style="section"}

## Rundle saves the Cup {style="second"}

:::paragraphs{style="byline"}
By **Walter Jago** · at Gannet Park
:::

:::paragraphs{style="flush"}
Wexcombe Athletic are in the final of the County Cup, and they owe it to Tom Rundle. With the score at one goal each and four minutes left on a pitch like a ploughed field, the referee gave a penalty against the home side for a handball nobody else in the ground saw.
:::

::resource{id="keeper"}

Penrose of Treviskey placed the ball, took four paces back and struck it hard and low towards the right-hand post. Rundle, who is a carpenter by trade and the shortest goalkeeper in the league, went full length into the mud and held it against his chest. The crowd of 6,200, the largest at Gannet Park since the war, threw caps in the air, and one man was seen to throw his wife’s hat.

From the clearance Athletic broke down the left, and young Billy Trounce, who is seventeen, ran half the length of the field before shooting past the Treviskey goalkeeper for the winner. He was carried off the pitch at the final whistle by supporters who had climbed the railings, and did not get his boots back until Sunday.

The first half had belonged to Treviskey. Their centre-forward, Hendra, scored after nine minutes with a header from a corner, and only a fine save by Rundle from the same player, low down at the near post, kept the score at one goal to nil at the interval. Athletic were level ten minutes after the restart, when Opie, son of the clockmaker, turned in a cross from Trounce at the second attempt.

The final, against Penhallow Rovers, will be played at Gannet Park on the 24th, kick-off at three. The club asks supporters to come early and to leave their wives’ hats at home.

## Rovers come by the long road {style="third"}

Penhallow Rovers, Athletic’s opponents in the final, have needed three replays to get there. They drew with St Just twice, the second time after extra time in a thunderstorm, and beat them at the third attempt at Camborne, where the match was stopped for a quarter of an hour while a horse was led off the pitch. Their semi-final against Porthleven was settled by a goal from their captain, Arthur Kitto, the Penhallow blacksmith, who is forty-one and says this will be his last season, as he has said for the past four.

Rovers have the stronger side on paper, with three men who have played for the county, and they beat Athletic twice in the league this winter. But they have never won the Cup, and they have never played at Gannet Park, where the slope from the town end is worth a goal to anyone who knows it. Athletic’s trainer, Mr Sam Rowe, has asked the groundsman not to cut the grass this week.

## The Harbour Mile {style="third"}

The Harbour Mile, run on Whit Monday along the Strand from the Custom House to the lifeboat slip and back, was won for the third year running by Jack Penrose of the Wexcombe Harriers in 4 minutes 31 seconds, a record for the course. He was followed home by his brother Harry, eleven yards behind, and by a visiting runner from Truro, who said that he had been told the course was flat. Forty-four ran, the youngest fourteen and the oldest sixty-two, and every one of them finished, though two had to be helped up the slip. The new trams were stopped for twenty minutes while the race passed, the first time, said the Borough Engineer, that the line has given way to anybody.

## Swimming club’s new raft {style="third"}

The Wexcombe Amateur Swimming Club has moored a new diving raft off West Sands, forty yards out and built by members over the winter from timber and oil drums given by the Harbour Board. It was christened on Sunday by the club’s oldest member, Mrs Grace Tonkin, who is seventy-three and swam out to it. The club’s summer gala is on the 15th of July; races for juniors, ladies and veterans, and a novelty race in fancy dress.

## Cricket {style="third"}

::resource{id="scores"}

Wexcombe beat St Austell by 41 runs at the Rec on Saturday. Dunstan’s 64, his first fifty of the season, carried the town to 168, and the Rev. A. Carne took five wickets for 22 with his slow left-arm bowling, which St Austell found as hard to read as his sermons.

## Regatta and bowls {style="third"}

The Harbour Regatta is fixed for Saturday the 1st of July, with gig racing, a swimming race across the harbour mouth and the greasy pole. Entries to the Secretary at the Harbour Office by the 24th. At bowls, Wexcombe Park beat Camborne by nine shots in the County League; Mr W. Oates, aged eighty-one, was again the best player on the green, and was presented afterwards with a new set of woods to mark his sixtieth season with the club. He said that he would keep the old ones for wet days.

## Wheelers’ fifty-mile trial {style="third"}

The Wexcombe Wheelers held their fifty-mile time trial on Sunday morning over the coast road to Hayle and back, starting at six o’clock to have the road to themselves. Twenty-three riders started at one-minute intervals and twenty finished; two punctured at Gwithian and one stopped, by his own account, for breakfast. The winner, Mr Leonard Uren, a postman, beat the club record by four minutes on a machine he built himself from parts, and was back at his round by nine.

::resource{id="wheelers"}

The club’s next event is the hill climb up Quay Hill on the 25th, which the Watch Committee has allowed on the condition that the riders keep clear of the tram lines, and the trams of the riders.

## Racing at Penhallow {style="third"}

The Whitsun meeting at Penhallow Downs drew a crowd of nine thousand on Monday, many of whom came by the special trains from Wexcombe. The big race, the Downs Plate over a mile and a half, went to *Harbour Lights*, owned by Mr Jory of Trelask and ridden by his nephew, at a hundred to eight. The favourite unseated his rider at the second hurdle and finished the race alone, to great applause, a good length ahead of the field.

The bookmakers had a poor afternoon. Three favourites were beaten, and the last race, the Farmers’ Handicap, was won by a grey mare from a farm above Wexcombe that had been ploughing on Saturday and had never run on a course before. Her owner, Mr Tom Oliver, said that he had entered her for a lark and backed her with half a crown, and that he would now buy a new plough with the winnings. The meeting closes on Wednesday with the Hunters’ Steeplechase, and the Great Western will again run special trains from Wexcombe at half past eleven and a quarter past twelve, returning after the last race. The Lantern’s tipster, who has not had a winner since Easter, refuses to name a horse.
