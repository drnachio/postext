## Trams run to the sea at last {style="lead"}

*Mayor Edith Rowe opens the new line before four thousand people, and drives the first car down Quay Hill herself*
:::

:::paragraphs{style="byline"}
By **Arthur Penhale** · Municipal Correspondent
:::

:::paragraphs{style="flush"}
The trams reached the harbour yesterday, eleven years after the Corporation first promised them. At a quarter past eleven the Mayor, Mrs Edith Rowe, cut a ribbon of green silk across the rails at the foot of Quay Hill, and a crowd that stretched back past the Fish Market cheered loud enough to lift the gulls off the warehouse roofs.
:::

::resource{id="mayor"}

“It has taken us longer than the Romans took to build their roads,” Mrs Rowe told the crowd from a platform dressed in bunting, “and our road is only a mile and three furlongs long. But it is ours, it is paid for, and from Monday a docker in Saltings Row can be at the Town Hall in nine minutes for a penny.”

The Harbour line leaves the main route at Market Cross and runs down Quay Hill, along the Strand and out to a new turning loop beside the lifeboat house. Four cars, rebuilt in the Corporation’s own works at Fennick Lane, will run every eight minutes from six in the morning until half past eleven at night.

The line cost £41,300, of which £12,000 came from the government’s grant for public works. Some two hundred men of the town were taken on to lay it, most of them from the register at the Labour Exchange, and the Borough Engineer, Mr Lionel Cardew, said that not one day had been lost to accident since the first sett was lifted in October.

## Miss Lacey lands on the Common {style="second"}

:::paragraphs{style="flush"}
The aviator Miss Constance Lacey brought her biplane down on Wexcombe Common at twenty past six on Wednesday evening, at the end of a flight from Croydon of three hours and fifty minutes. She came in low over the cricket pavilion, scattering a game of the Wednesday Eleven, and rolled to a stop forty yards from the bandstand.
:::


Miss Lacey, who is twenty-four, is flying round the coast of England and Wales in stages to raise money for the lifeboat fund, and has so far raised £1,108. “The Common is the best field I have met since Kent,” she said, “though the cows took some persuading.”

Her machine is a two-seater of 1929 with a Gipsy engine, bought second-hand from a flying club in Hampshire and named *Kittiwake*. She navigates with a road map folded on her knee, and follows the railway lines where the coast turns misty. “The Great Western has never once let me down,” she said. “I cannot say the same for the weather.”

She stayed the night at the Mermaid, where the landlord refused to take her money, and left at seven the next morning for Penzance, waving to a crowd of schoolchildren who had been let out of lessons to see her off. Two of them had sat up all night on the pavilion roof to be sure of a good view. The pavilion has asked for a signed photograph for its wall, and the Wednesday Eleven for a replay.

### The weather

Fair at first, with a fresh breeze from the west; cloud later and a little rain in the evening, clearing overnight. Sea moderate. Sunrise 4.58, sunset 9.31. Yesterday’s highest temperature in the town was 68 degrees, at the Fish Market at three o’clock, and the lowest 51, at the coastguard station at dawn. The Harbour Master reports the best visibility of the year from Gannet Point, where the Stags could be seen plainly at noon.

**Lighthouse dark for a night.** The Gannet Point light will be shut down on Tuesday from dusk to midnight while its new lamp is fitted. Ships are warned by the Harbour Master.

**Choir to Cardiff.** The Wexcombe Male Voice Choir sails on Thursday for the Eisteddfod. Forty-two singers, one harmonium and the conductor’s spaniel will make the crossing.

# Town & Harbour {style="section"}

## Trams: a hill too steep, they said {style="second"}

:::paragraphs{style="byline"}
Continued from page 1
:::

Many doubted that a tram could manage Quay Hill at all. The gradient is one in eleven at its worst, and a petition of 1928 warned of runaway cars and ruin in the Fish Market. Mr Cardew’s answer was a track brake of his own design, tried on the hill for six weeks this spring with cars loaded with sandbags.

Mrs Rowe silenced the doubters in the simplest way. After the ribbon was cut she climbed to the front platform of car No. 9, took the controller from the motorman and drove the first car down the hill herself, at a steady walking pace and to a roar from the pavements. She is believed to be the first Mayor of Wexcombe to drive anything at all.

## The clock that stopped for nine years strikes again {style="second"}

:::paragraphs{style="byline"}
By **Mary Tregarthen**
:::

:::paragraphs{style="flush"}
For nine years the clock on the Guildhall tower has said twenty to four. Yesterday, at noon precisely, it said twelve, and the bell in the cupola struck the hour for the first time since the great gale of 1924 bent its hammer and cracked the escapement wheel.
:::

::resource{id="clock"}

The work was done by Mr Samuel Opie, clockmaker, of Chapel Street, and his two sons, who have climbed the tower every evening since February. “The wheel was cast in 1799, and nobody casts them like that now,” said Mr Opie. “So we cast it like that ourselves.” The new hammer was forged at the Harbour Works from an old capstan bar.

A small crowd had gathered in Guildhall Square by a quarter to twelve, many of them with their watches out. When the first stroke sounded there was a moment of silence, and then a cheer, and Mr Opie’s younger son was seen to wave his cap from the louvres of the belfry.

The Town Clerk said that the old joke, that Wexcombe was the only town in England where it was always teatime, would now have to be retired. The pigeons, he added, would have to get used to the bell.

## A cod the size of a boy {style="second"}

:::paragraphs{style="flush"}
The largest cod landed at Wexcombe in living memory came ashore on Thursday in the hold of the *Morning Star*, skipper Jacob Hosking, 71. It weighed 82 pounds on the Fish Market scales and measured five feet and an inch from its nose to the fork of its tail.
:::

::resource{id="fisherman"}

“I’ve fished these grounds since I was eleven,” Mr Hosking said, “and I never saw his like. He fought for three-quarters of an hour, and I reckon he’d have won if my grandson hadn’t been there to hold my belt.”

The fish was sold to Messrs Kitto of the Strand for a guinea and is to be shown in their window, packed in ice, until Monday. A photograph has been sent to the Natural History Museum, which has asked whether the skipper might part with the head. Mr Hosking says he will think about it.

## Council votes for an open-air bathing pool {style="second"}

The Town Council on Tuesday voted by eighteen votes to seven to build an open-air bathing pool at Gannet Point, in the hollow of the old quarry below the coastguard cottages. The pool will be filled by the tide through a sluice, and will measure fifty yards by twenty, with diving stages and a café.

The Borough Engineer puts the cost at £9,600. Councillor Bastian, who led the opposition, said the town already had a perfectly good sea. Councillor Mrs Pascoe replied that the sea was perfectly good only in August, and that children had been drowned in it.

Work is to begin in October, so that the pool may open for the summer of 1934. Seventy men will be employed.

## Lifeboat out twice in one night {style="second"}

The Wexcombe lifeboat *Sarah Jolly* was launched twice on Tuesday night, the first time at a quarter to eleven to the yacht *Petrel*, whose engine had failed off Gannet Point with three men aboard, and the second at half past two to a fishing boat reported overdue from the mackerel grounds.

The *Petrel* was towed into harbour shortly after midnight. The fishing boat, the *Brothers’ Pride*, was found at anchor in the lee of the Stags, her crew asleep. “They had a good catch and saw no reason to hurry,” said the coxswain, Mr William Bassett. “I told them what I thought of that, and then I gave them a tow, because the tide was turning.”

The crew of twelve were back in their beds by five. It was the boat’s fourteenth service this year.

## Fire at the rope works {style="second"}

A fire broke out in the tarring shed of Messrs Pengelly’s rope works on Fennick Lane shortly after four on Thursday afternoon, and for an hour threatened the whole of the long rope walk beside it. The alarm was given by a boy delivering newspapers, who saw smoke coming from under the door and ran to the fire station in Market Street, a distance of three hundred yards, in what the Chief Officer called the best time he had ever seen.

The brigade had two jets on the shed within ten minutes. The roof fell in at a quarter to five, but the fire was kept from the rope walk, where some forty coils of new manila rope were stored for the harbour, and the walls of the shed are standing. Mr Pengelly puts the damage at £300, and says that work will go on as usual on Monday, in the open air if it must.

The boy, Jimmy Rowse, aged twelve, has been given a reward of five shillings by the firm and a ride on the engine by the brigade. He said he preferred the ride.

:::callout{type="info" title="Harbour line: what you need to know"}
Cars run every eight minutes from Market Cross to the Lifeboat House, calling at Quay Hill, the Fish Market, the Custom House and Strand Corner.

The fare is one penny all the way, a halfpenny for children under fourteen. Workmen’s returns at three-halfpence are sold before eight in the morning. On Sundays cars start at nine.
:::
