# Where the mud meets the reeds {kicker="Winter survey · The upper bay" lead="From October to March the observatory counted the birds of the Wrenmarsh shore at every high tide and ringed in the reedbed on calm mornings. Four species tell most of what changed this winter."}

The upper bay at Wrenmarsh is a strip of ground less than a kilometre wide, between the last of the open mud and the first stand of common reed. At low water it is a plain of grey silt cut by creeks. Two hours before high water the tide fills the creeks from below, the mud goes under in twenty minutes, and every wader on the flats has to find somewhere to spend the next four hours. Most of them come to the saltmarsh at the reed edge, and that is where we count them.

This winter’s survey ran from 4 October to 28 March, with a count at every daylight high tide above 4.2 metres: 96 counts in all, made by eleven volunteers from the two hides on the sea wall. In the reedbed behind them we worked the same three net rides as in the last nine winters, on 41 mornings with wind under 12 knots. The figures below are the observatory’s own.

## The curlew roost

The curlew is the bird the upper bay is known for, and the one we worry about most. Our peak this winter was 1,240 on 19 January, on a spring tide of 4.9 metres that pushed the whole flock off the low marsh and onto the grazed field behind the sea wall (:ref{id="curlew" case="lower"}). That is 180 more than last winter’s peak, and the highest count since 2017.

Two things lie behind it. The first is the weather. A cold spell in the second week of January froze the inland fields where many curlews feed in winter, and birds that would have stayed on the pasture came down to the coast, where the mud does not freeze. The second is the roost itself. In 2024 the field behind the wall was taken out of winter grazing at the observatory’s request, and the grass, cut short in September, gives a flock a clear view on every side. Curlews will not roost where a fox can come within thirty metres unseen.

We read 63 colour rings on curlews this winter, 22 of them our own. The others came from Finland, Sweden and the Netherlands, and one bird ringed as a chick in Lapland in 2016 has now spent ten winters on this shore.

## Redshank on the creeks

Redshank behave differently. They feed in the creeks rather than on the open mud and leave last when the tide comes in, so a count at high water finds them spread along every channel instead of packed into one roost. Our peak was 410 in November (:ref{id="redshank" case="lower"}), close to the ten-year average. The birds of the upper bay are a mixture of Icelandic visitors, which arrive in September and leave in April, and local breeders, which spend the winter a few kilometres from the wet meadows where they nest.

The two can be told apart in the hand. Icelandic birds have longer wings and a darker, browner back; our breeders are paler and greyer. Of the 34 redshank caught in the creek nets this winter, 21 were of the Icelandic type, and three had first been ringed as adults more than ten years ago. A redshank that survives its first winter can live to twenty.

## A reedbed with tenants

Bearded reedlings are the birds visitors ask about, and the hardest to see. They spend the winter in small flocks deep in the reedbed, feeding on the seeds of the reed heads, and come to the edge only on still mornings, when their metallic *ping* carries across the water. This winter they had their best season since the reedbed was extended in 2019 (:ref{id="reedling" case="lower"}). We caught 58, against an average of 19, and saw flocks of more than thirty on four dates in February.

The cutting regime explains most of it. Every winter since 2019 a fifth of the reedbed has been cut, in a rotation of blocks, so that the bed always holds reed of every age from one to five years. Reedlings nest low, in old reed with a tangle of fallen stems, and feed on seed from the tall young growth. A bed of one age suits them for only part of the year.

## Late warblers

Reed warblers should be in West Africa by October, and most of them are. Three stayed late this year. The last was caught on 11 October in the middle net ride (:ref{id="warbler" case="lower"}), an adult in fresh plumage with a fat score of four, enough fuel for a flight of a thousand kilometres or more. Late birds of this kind have turned up in four of the last six autumns, against two in the twenty years before, and the dates are getting later. Whether they bred late or are changing their timing, three rings cannot tell us.
