# Choosing the ground

An apple tree planted today will still be bearing when the person who planted it has stopped climbing ladders. The choice of ground is the one decision that cannot be put right later, and in a valley where the frost comes in September and stays until May it decides more than the choice of variety does.

## Cold air runs downhill

On a still night the air that cools against the ground grows heavy and slides down the slope, the way water would, and pools wherever the land stops falling: in the valley floor, behind a hedge, against a wall at the bottom of a field. Two trees a hundred metres apart can see five degrees of difference on a May morning, and five degrees is the gap between a crop and a tree in flower that sets nothing.

Plant on the slope, never in the hollow below it. A third of the way up is usually the warmest band, above the frost pocket and below the wind. If the orchard has to sit low, leave a gap in the bottom hedge so the cold air can drain away instead of backing up into the trees.

## Facing the sun

A slope that faces south or south-west warms first in spring and ripens the wood in autumn, and ripe wood is what carries a tree through a hard winter. A north slope delays blossom by a week or more, which can save it from a late frost, but the fruit may not colour before the first snow. In this valley the south-west slopes above the river have always carried the old orchards, and the farmhouses below them sit just above the line where the fog settles.

## Soil and water

Apples want deep soil that drains. A tree will put up with poor ground, but it will not put up with its roots standing in water through the winter: the roots die back, the bark at the collar rots, and the tree goes yellow the following summer for no reason anyone can see. Dig a hole sixty centimetres deep in March and fill it with water. If the water is still there the next day, the ground needs draining or the tree needs a mound to stand on.

Heavy clay holds water and cold; it also holds nutrients, and an apple on clay crops for a long time once it is established. Light sand warms early and drains well but dries out in August, when the fruit is swelling. Most valley soils sit somewhere between the two, and the rootstock, the subject of the next chapter, does more to match a tree to its soil than any amount of digging.
