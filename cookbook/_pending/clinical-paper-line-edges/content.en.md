# Point-of-care lung ultrasound for community-acquired pneumonia in children: a prospective diagnostic accuracy study {style="article" issue="2026 · 9(4) · 211–213" verso="Ribera Paediatr J 2026;9(4)" recto="Barreiro Soto et al. · Lung ultrasound for pneumonia" kind="Original research" authors="Inés Barreiro Soto^1^, Tomasz Wierzbicki^2^, Aisha Mensah^1^, Pablo Lago Fernández^3^" affiliations="^1^ Paediatric Emergency Department, Hospital Comarcal de la Ribera\n^2^ Department of Radiology, Hospital Comarcal de la Ribera\n^3^ Clinical Epidemiology Unit, Ribera Health Area" note="Illustrative article written for the Postext Cookbook: the study, its authors and its data are invented. The works cited are real."}

:::callout{type="abstract" title="Abstract"}
**Objective** To find out how accurately emergency paediatricians diagnose community-acquired pneumonia in children with lung ultrasound, against chest radiography read by two radiologists and a review on day 14.

**Methods** We enrolled 412 children aged 3 months to 15 years who came to one emergency department with fever and cough or fast breathing. One of nine paediatricians scanned each child before the radiograph, after a six-hour course.

**Results** Pneumonia was diagnosed in 139 children (33.7%). Ultrasound had a sensitivity of 91.4% (95% CI 85.5 to 95.0) and a specificity of 95.2% (92.0 to 97.2), so LR+ = 19.19 and LR− = 0.09. A median scan took 6 minutes.

**Conclusions** After a short course, emergency paediatricians rule pneumonia in or out with ultrasound about as well as a radiograph does, and without radiation.
:::

## Introduction

Pneumonia is the commonest serious infection of childhood and the reason for many of the chest radiographs taken in children’s emergency departments. British guidelines advise against a routine radiograph in a child well enough to go home [@harris2011], yet the film is often asked for to settle a doubtful examination, and two radiologists reading the same film do not always agree.

Lung ultrasound looks at the same disease through another window. Consolidated lung carries sound and shows as a solid, liver-like area with bright air bronchograms, signs that were described and standardised in adults first [@lichtenstein2008; @volpicelli2012]. In children the thin chest wall makes the scan easier, and a meta-analysis and a large prospective study found it both sensitive and specific [@pereda2015; @shah2013]. Most of those scans, however, were done by sonographers or by a few physicians with long experience, and it is not known whether the figures hold when the scan is one task among many on a night shift.

We measured the accuracy of lung ultrasound done by the emergency paediatricians of a district hospital after a short course, and report it as STARD 2015 asks [@bossuyt2015].

## Methods

### Participants

From 1 October 2024 to 30 November 2025 we approached children aged 3 months to 15 years who came to the emergency department of a district hospital of 280 beds with a temperature of 38 °C or more and cough, fast breathing or chest indrawing. Children with chronic lung disease, congenital heart disease or a radiograph taken elsewhere in the previous week were not included. Parents gave written consent, and the regional ethics committee approved the protocol. Data were entered in REDCap [@harris2009].

### Index test

Nine paediatricians, none of whom had used lung ultrasound before, attended a course of six hours, two of them spent scanning under supervision. Each scan covered six zones on each side of the chest with a linear probe of 8 to 12 MHz and was recorded as positive when a consolidation of 1 cm or more with air bronchograms was seen under the pleura. The scan was done before the radiograph, and the paediatrician entered the result before seeing the film.

### Reference standard

Two radiologists who did not know the ultrasound result read every radiograph, and a third settled disagreements. The final diagnosis joined the agreed reading to a telephone review on day 14, as STARD advises when no single test is a gold standard [@bossuyt2015]. A child with a normal film who was given antibiotics for pneumonia by the family doctor within the 14 days was counted as having pneumonia.

### Statistical analysis

Sensitivity and specificity are given with Wilson intervals [@newcombe1998], and likelihood ratios with the intervals of Simel and colleagues [@simel1991]. We read a positive likelihood ratio above 10, or a negative one below 0.1, as a large change in the probability of disease [@altman1994; @deeks2004]. Agreement was measured with Cohen’s kappa and read on the scale of Landis and Koch [@landis1977]. We planned 400 children, so that with a prevalence of one in three the lower limit of the interval for a sensitivity of 90% would stay above 83%. A value of p < 0.05 was taken as significant.

## Results

Of 451 children screened, 412 were enrolled. Their mean age was 4.1 ± 3.2 years, 221 (53.6%) were boys and 97 (23.5%) were under 2 years. Pneumonia was diagnosed in 139 (33.7%), 14 of them only at the review on day 14.

Ultrasound was positive in 140 children and right in 127 of them. Its sensitivity was 91.4% (95% CI 85.5 to 95.0) and its specificity 95.2% (92.0 to 97.2), with a positive predictive value of 90.7% and a negative predictive value of 95.6%. The likelihood ratios were LR+ = 19.19 and LR− = 0.09, both past the limits set in advance. Sensitivity was lower in children under 2 years, 31 of 35 (88.6%), than in older children, 96 of 104 (92.3%), a difference of the size chance would give (p = 0.51). Sensitivity also rose with practice, from 87.5% in each paediatrician’s first 20 scans to 94.0% after them (p = 0.18).

The 12 false negatives were consolidations under 1 cm on the film (7), shadows behind the heart (3) and two films read as pneumonia by both radiologists in children whose fever settled without antibiotics. In a logistic model, a consolidation under 1 cm on the film was the only factor linked to a missed diagnosis (OR = 6.2, 95% CI 2.1 to 18.4; p < 0.001); an age under 2 years was not (OR = 1.6; p = 0.43). Between the paediatricians and an expert who reviewed 120 stored clips, kappa = 0.84; between the two radiologists, kappa = 0.71. The median scan took 6 minutes (interquartile range 4 to 8), and no child needed sedation.

## Discussion

With six hours of training, emergency paediatricians diagnosed pneumonia with ultrasound about as accurately as the experienced operators of earlier studies [@pereda2015; @shah2013], and agreed with an expert more closely than the radiologists agreed with each other. With LR+ = 19.19, a positive scan in a child whose pre-test probability is one in three raises it above 90%, and with LR− = 0.09 a negative scan brings it under 5%.

The false negatives show where the scan is weak: small consolidations and the lung behind the heart, which the probe cannot reach between the ribs. They also show the weakness of the reference standard, since two of them were probably shadows on the film. The study has other limits. It was done in one hospital, the paediatricians volunteered for the course, and a review by telephone on day 14 will have missed some mild cases.

The scan suits the place where the decision is taken. A child who would otherwise be sent to radiology can be scanned in the cubicle, and the radiograph kept for the child whose scan is doubtful or whose fever does not settle as expected.
