# Appendix C · Varieties for a short season

Varieties that ripen before the first hard frost and stand a valley winter, in order of picking.

**Yellow Transparent.** From the Baltic; reached America in 1870. Pale, soft, picked in early August. Will not keep: for sauce within the week.

**Duchess of Oldenburg.** Russian, known since the eighteenth century. Striped, sharp, picked in August. A cooking apple from a very hardy tree.

**Antonovka.** Russian and very old. Large, green, sour and scented. Cooks to a froth; keeps until Christmas. The hardiest tree on this list.

**Wealthy.** Raised from seed in Minnesota by Peter Gideon in the 1860s. Red, crisp, for eating in September.

**Lobo.** A McIntosh seedling released at Ottawa in 1930. Dark red, soft, sweet. Picked in mid-September.

**McIntosh.** Found as a seedling in Ontario in 1811. Scented, white-fleshed, tender. Hardy, but scab spots the fruit in wet summers.

**Fameuse.** Also called Snow, for its white flesh. Grown in Quebec since the eighteenth century. Probably a parent of McIntosh.

**Liberty.** From the Geneva station in New York, 1978. Bred to resist scab: the tree for an orchard that is never sprayed.

**Honeycrisp.** Minnesota, released in 1991. Very crisp and juicy; keeps for months. Cracks and spots on some soils.

**Haralson.** Minnesota, 1922. Tart, firm, for cooking and eating. One of the hardiest trees that carry a good keeper.

**Belle de Boskoop.** Dutch, from 1856. Russeted, sharp, large. Keeps until March. A triploid: plant two other varieties to pollinate it, and do not use its pollen for them.

**Northern Spy.** New York, about 1800. Slow to start cropping, then excellent. Firm, aromatic, the best keeper on the list.
