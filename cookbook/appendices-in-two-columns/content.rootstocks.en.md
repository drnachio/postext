# Rootstocks and spacing

An apple does not come true from seed. Every named variety is grafted: a bud or a shoot of the variety is joined to the roots of another apple, and those roots decide how large the tree will grow, how soon it will crop and how well it stands cold, wet or drought. The variety decides the fruit. The rootstock decides almost everything else.

## Small trees, early fruit

Most rootstocks in use today come from the research station at East Malling, in Kent, and carry its initial: M9, M26, MM106. The lower the vigour, the sooner the tree bears and the smaller it stays. A tree on M9 may give a few apples in its second summer and will stand about two and a half metres tall, small enough to prune and pick from the ground. It pays for that with a weak root system: it needs a stake for the whole of its life and good soil to grow in.

## Large trees, long lives

A tree on a vigorous rootstock such as MM111 or M25 takes five or six years to crop and grows into a tree that needs a ladder, but it roots deep, finds water in a dry summer and will stand in grass without help. These are the trees of the old orchards on the slopes, some of them older than the farmhouses. Where sheep graze under the branches, or where nobody will water a young tree in August, a vigorous stock is the safer choice.

## The cold

Hardiness belongs to the rootstock as much as to the variety. The Malling stocks were raised for English winters, and in a valley that drops below minus twenty-five some of them suffer. The Budagovsky stocks, bred in central Russia, were raised for that cold: Bud 9 makes a tree the size of one on M9 and comes through winters that kill M9 roots. Seedlings of Antonovka, an old Russian apple, give a full-sized tree that is hardier still.

## How far apart

Spacing follows from the rootstock. Trees on M9 can stand a metre and a half apart in rows three and a half metres wide; on MM106, four metres apart; on M25, eight or more.
