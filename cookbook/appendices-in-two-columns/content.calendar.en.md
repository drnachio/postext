# Appendix B · The orchard year

The months are those of this valley, where the last frost can come in late May and the first in late September. Higher up, everything moves a week or two later.

**January.** Prune on days when the wood is not frozen, starting with the oldest trees. Check the guards round young trunks: voles work under snow, and hares strip bark when the ground is white.

**February.** Finish the winter pruning of standards. Take scion wood for grafting from last summer's shoots, label it and keep it wrapped and cool until April.

**March.** Plant bare-root trees as soon as the soil can be worked and is not sticky. Stake trees on dwarfing stocks the day they go in.

**April.** Graft when the buds of the rootstock begin to open. Keep a thermometer in the lowest part of the orchard and watch the forecast: open blossom is killed at about minus two.

**May.** Blossom, a week later on the north slopes. Leave the grass long under the trees until the bees have finished. On a clear, still night after a cold day, expect frost before dawn.

**June.** After the natural drop at the end of the month, thin the fruit to one per cluster, and to one every fifteen centimetres on young trees.

**July.** Water young trees in dry spells, a full bucket each, once a week.

**August.** Summer-prune cordons and espaliers once the new shoots are woody at the base. Pick the early varieties when they part from the spur with a lift and a twist.

**September.** The main picking begins. Handle the fruit as you would eggs, and store only sound apples, in a cool, dark, airy place.

**October.** Finish picking before the hard frosts. Rake up fallen leaves where scab was bad: the fungus spends the winter in them.

**November.** Plant and move trees while the soil is still warm. Paint the trunks of young trees white against the winter sun, which warms the bark by day and lets it crack at night.

**December.** Go through the store once a week and take out anything that has started to rot.
