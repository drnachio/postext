# Composed-page books

**You build one page of a textbook from a template, fill it with text from the Markdown, label a map, translate the page and fix what does not fit; then you add a tree, notes on a map, a page of photo cards and a module library.**

A page of a school textbook is a set of boxes: a title band, a text, a box for a definition, a box for an activity, a map. Postext lays such a page out from a **page template** that holds the boxes (its frames), and the Markdown only says which words go in which frame. This page walks through the whole job in the Sandbox, on a book that already exists, and adds one page to it. The reference pages say what each field and each line of Markdown does; here you do each thing once, in order, and see what it changes: [Composed pages](/en/docs/composed-pages) for the Markdown, [Configuration › Composed pages](/en/docs/configuration-composed-pages) for the template, [Sandbox: composed pages](/en/docs/sandbox-composed-pages) for the screens.

You need a browser and nothing else. Every step happens in the Sandbox, and what you change stays in your browser.

## 1. Open the book

The book is *Our river*, a unit of a Year 3 textbook that the [Cookbook](/en/cookbook/children-textbook-pages) publishes as recipe Nº 156. The river, the towns and the pictures are invented for it. It has nine pages, with a painted map across two facing ones, and it comes in Spanish, Catalan and English.

[Open it in the Sandbox](/en/sandbox#recipe=children-textbook-pages&lang=en). The first visit adds it to **Books** as one of your own books, so you can change it freely; with `lang=es` or `lang=ca` in the link you get the other editions. If you come back to the same link later, the Sandbox asks whether to open your copy or to replace it with the published one.

Two things to know before you touch anything:

- The bar above the preview has a switch with **Content** and **Design**. In Content mode you edit words, in the **Text** panel or on the page. In Design mode you edit page templates and styles, and the text is read only. **Alt+Shift+M** (**⌥⇧M** on a Mac) switches between them.
- The **Text** panel shows one chapter of Markdown. Each composed page is a block that starts with `::::page{template="…"}` and ends with four colons. Click some words on a page in the preview and the caret goes to them in the Markdown.

## 2. Make a page template

The new page will be a review page: a title band, a short text under it and two boxes, one to recall what was learned and one to try. It needs a template with four frames.

1. Switch to **Design** and open the **Design** panel on the left. Choose **Page & columns**, then **Page templates**. The list shows each template of the book with a drawing of its frames.
2. Press **New**. A template called *New template* opens. Give it the **ID** `review` (the name the Markdown will use) and the **Name** `Review page` (what this panel shows). The ID takes lowercase letters, digits and dashes.
3. The template starts with one frame, `text`, that **Takes the page's loose text**. Make it the title band: **Name (slot)**: `title`; **Box style**: `portada`, the blue band the book already uses for its titles; switch **Takes the page's loose text** off; **Takes**: *Level 1*, so that this frame receives the page's first `#` heading.
4. Press **Add frame**. The new frame opens below the list of frames. **Name (slot)**: `text`, and switch **Takes the page's loose text** on: whatever the page writes outside a frame goes here.
5. Add a frame. **Name (slot)**: `remember`. **Box style**: `dato`, a tinted box with a tab. **Smallest height**: 30 mm, so that the box keeps a good size when its text is short.
6. Add a last frame. **Name (slot)**: `try`. **Box style**: `actividad`, a box with a stripe down its side. **Smallest height**: 30 mm.
7. In `text`, `remember` and `try` set **Width** to *Up to the edge* and **Height** to *Its content*: they then fill the width of the page and are as tall as their text.
8. Under **Groups** press **Add group**. Name it `column`, leave it *Stacked* and set **Space between frames** to 6 mm. Under **Members, in page order** tick the four frames. Tick them in the order they go, the title first: the list numbers the members and the arrows move one up or down. A group lays its frames one under the other and moves the ones below when a frame grows, so a longer text in the first box pushes the second box down instead of running over it.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/template.webp" alt="The Design panel open at Page templates, showing the Review page template: its four frames and its group, and the same frames drawn with dashed outlines on the page." caption="The template: four frames and one group. On the page, the dashed boxes are the frames and the green labels say how each one is sized." />

The template has no words in it. Nothing you set says what the title is or what the boxes contain; the box style gives a frame its look, and the group gives the frames their order. There are other ways to the same template. In Design mode you can draw a frame on an empty spot of a composed page with the mouse and move or resize the frames you have (see [Sandbox: composed pages](/en/docs/sandbox-composed-pages#page-templates)); **Duplicate** copies a template to change it; **Save layout as template** turns a composed page you like into a new template.

## 3. Fill the page from the Markdown

1. Switch to **Content**, open the **Text** panel and press **Ctrl+End** (**Cmd+↓** on a Mac) to go to the end of the chapter.
2. Press **Composed page** in the toolbar of the Text panel. A picker lists the page templates of the book, each drawn as its empty frames. Choose *Review page*.
3. The Sandbox writes the page for you, one empty frame per slot, and selects the word `title` of the heading. Type the title over it.

```md
::::page{template="review"}
# Check what you have learned

A river changes as it runs from the mountains to the sea. Read the boxes, then answer them in your own words.

:::frame{slot="remember" label="Remember"}
A spring gives a trickle, streams join into a river, and the river widens as it nears the sea.
:::

:::frame{slot="try" label="Try it"}
Draw a river from its spring to the sea. Write the names of the three courses on your drawing.
:::
::::
```

The heading goes to the frame that takes level 1, the paragraph that follows it to the frame that takes the loose text, and each `:::frame` to the frame of its `slot`. The `label` is the text on the tab of a box. The page is on the canvas as page 10, after the nine of the unit: a composed page always starts a new page.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/fill.webp" alt="The Text panel with the Markdown of the new page, and the finished page beside it with a blue title, a paragraph and two boxes with tabs." caption="The page in the Markdown and on the canvas." />

Notice what the Markdown does not say: no position, no size, no colour. Those belong to the template, and the same Markdown laid out with another template would look different.

## 4. Label the map

Pages 6 and 7 hold a painted map across the spread. Its words are not part of the picture. Each name on it is a line of the Markdown, inside the `:::figure` of the map:

```md
:::figure{resource="mapa" slot="mapa"}
::label[Spring]{at="nacimiento"}
::label[Molinar]{at="molinar"}
::label[Lower course]{at="bajo"}
:::
```

`at` names an **anchor**: a point of the picture, defined once in the picture's resource and shared by every language. To add the stone bridge of Molinar:

1. In the Text panel go to the map's figure (search for `at="bajo"`) and add a line under the last label: `::label[Stone bridge]{at="bridge"}`.
2. Open **Checks**. The list has a *Label at an unknown anchor* entry for page 6: the picture has no anchor called `bridge`. Press the pin button on the entry, *Place the anchor bridge on the picture*.
3. A dialog shows the map with the anchors it has. The name `bridge` is picked under *Labels without an anchor*. Click the picture where the bridge stands.
4. Set the **Label style** of the new anchor to `pueblo`, the style of the towns, and press **Save**.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/anchor.webp" alt="The Anchors dialog: the map with its points, the new anchor named bridge selected, and its Label style field." caption="A click on the picture adds the anchor; its label style makes it look like the names of the towns." />

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/map.webp" alt="The map across pages 6 and 7 with the new label Stone bridge on the bridge, beside the labels of the towns." caption="The label is set at the point you clicked, in the book's own fonts." />

The word *Stone bridge* is Markdown; the point is part of the picture. When you translate the page, the same anchor gets another word.

## 5. Translate the page

A translation is the same template with other words. The Sandbox holds it as another edition of the same book: a toolbar at the left edge of the canvas switches between the editions, and the Checks panel reads all of them (see [Editions of a book](/en/docs/sandbox-previews#editions-of-a-book)). That toolbar shows when the pointer reaches the edge, and its pin button keeps it open. To make one:

1. Open **Books**, press **⋯** on your book and choose **Add a language…**. Pick the language and press **Add and open**. The Sandbox copies the edition on screen into a new one, sets its **Document language** to the language you picked and opens it. Hyphenation, quotation marks and the words the engine writes itself (such as *Continued* at the cut of a box) follow that language. The text is still the original's.
2. In the **Text** panel translate the words and leave the rest.

## 6. Review what does not fit

Words differ in length from one language to the next, and a box that held the English text may not hold the Spanish one. The Checks panel finds those frames. To see one, paste three or four more paragraphs of any text into the `remember` frame of the translated page, so that it holds more than the page has room for.

The page now shows the frame outlined in red with a band under its foot that says how much is missing (*26 mm short*). In **Checks**, under *Composed pages*, the entry *Text does not fit its frame* says what the layout tried before giving up (more room from the frames around, then smaller text) and lists the ways out as buttons:

- *let the text shrink to 82.5 %* lets the text of this frame go down to that size. It suits a few lines too many.
- *continue it on the next page* sends what does not fit to a page added after this one. It suits a longer text.
- *cut about 495 characters* changes nothing. It selects the text of the frame and tells you how much to cut.

A button that changes the template (and so every page set with it) asks first and names the setting. Press **Change it**. The page is laid out again, the box ends with the word that marks the cut (*Continúa*) and the rest runs on at the head of an added page. **Cmd/Ctrl+Z** takes the change back. **Reflow**, in the same toolbar at the edge of the canvas, opens *What moved and why*: the frames whose size or place the layout changed to fit their content, with the reason in words.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/checks.webp" alt="The Checks panel with the entry Text does not fit its frame and three buttons offering fixes, and the page with its first box outlined in red and a band saying 26 mm short." caption="A frame that does not fit, and the fixes the layout worked out for it." />

Not every mark on the canvas is a fault. A frame whose text or pictures the layout set smaller, within what its template allows, has a dashed amber outline and a badge such as *text at 90 %*: that is information. The problems are drawn in red (a frame whose content does not fit) and violet (a look the design does not have). **Fit marks**, in the same toolbar as **Reflow**, goes through three settings:

A book that sets nothing starts on **Problems only**: the scale badges show once you choose **All**. The Sandbox remembers your choice in this browser. The book can set it for whoever has not chosen: `debug.fitMarks` (`"all"`, `"problems"` or `"none"`) and `debug.fitMarksMinScale`, which leaves the badge off a frame set at that scale or larger (with `0.95` only what is under 95 % is marked). These marks belong to the canvas: the PDF, the HTML viewer, the EPUB and the Folio never draw them. Since postext 1.52.

A box with photos and their captions (frames anchored inside it, a caption `below` each photo) does not grow by itself when a translation makes the captions longer: they run out of the box and onto what lies under it. Open the box's frame in the template editor and set **Grows with the frames inside** to *The frames anchored inside it* (`growWith: "children"` in the template). The box then reaches the foot of the lowest caption and its bottom padding, its fill, border and tab follow, and the frames under it move down. *The frames chosen* takes a list of names instead. The frames anchored to a point of the box's top side count, and those anchored to them; a frame anchored to the box's foot or middle does not, and a turned box does not grow. Since postext 1.52.

## 7. Select on the page and in the text

The page and its Markdown follow each other.

- **From the page to the text.** Click on the words of a frame: the caret goes to that paragraph in the Text panel, and a path under the preview names where you are (*Page 10 › column › try › paragraph 1*). In Design mode the same click picks the frame for the template editor instead.
- **From the text to the page.** Put the caret in a paragraph: the paragraph and the frame that holds it are outlined on the page, and the same path says where they are.
- **From a check.** Click an entry of the Checks panel and the Sandbox takes you to the page, picks the frame and puts the caret at its text. In a book with several editions it switches to the edition that has the problem.

## 8. Draw a tree with connectors

A tree of ideas, an org chart and a timeline are boxes joined by lines. In Postext the boxes are frames and the lines are **connectors**: lines of the template's decoration that join frames. A connector is drawn after the frames have their final boxes, so it follows a box that grows with a longer text or with the words of another language, and the template needs no picture of the lines. You draw the tree once, as a **module** (a block of frames that any composed page can hold), and the Markdown says how many branches it has.

1. In **Design**, open **Page & columns › Modules** and press **New module**. A module called *Module* joins the list with one frame, `text`. Press its pencil (*Edit module Module*): the template editor opens in place, with the **Module** switch on.
2. Give it the **ID** `tree` and the **Name** `Tree of ideas`. Pick the frame `text` and make it the top of the tree: **Name (slot)** `root`, **Box style** `dato`, **Width** *Up to the edge*, **Height** *Its content*, and switch **Takes the page's loose text** off.
3. Press **Add frame**. **Name (slot)**: `branch`; **Box style**: `dato`; **Smallest height**: 20 mm.
4. Under **Groups** press **Add group**. Name it `branches` and choose **Grid**: **Columns** 3, **Gap between columns** and **Gap between rows** 6 mm. Under **Repeat a set of frames** choose `branch` as the **Main frame**: the grid lays one `branch` per item the page gives it. Set **Box of its own** to *Frame root*, **Relative position** *Below* and **Offset Y** 12 mm, so the grid starts under the root whatever its height.
5. Under **Decoration** press **Add connector**. In its card set **Starts at** to *Frame root* and, under **Goes to**, press *Every branch* (the ends a line has are pressed). Set **Route** to *Tree*, **Bend radius** to 2 mm, **Line width** to 1.5 pt and **End mark** to *Arrow*. **Pick on the page** does the same with the mouse, in Design mode: click the frame the line starts from, then each frame it goes to; a second click takes an end out, and Escape ends the picking.

Now the words. Switch to **Content** and put the caret on the review page of section 3 in the **Text** panel. Back in **Modules**, the **Page to insert into** list names that page; press the insert button of *Tree of ideas*. The Sandbox writes a `:::module{template="tree"}` block before the page's closing fence, with an empty frame for each slot, and adds a group that takes modules to the template `review`, under its last frame, because it had none. Write the words into the empty frames:

```md
:::module{template="tree"}
:::frame{slot="root"}
**A river** gives us
:::
:::frame{slot="branch"}
Water to drink and to water the fields
:::
:::frame{slot="branch"}
Power to turn a mill
:::
:::frame{slot="branch"}
A place to play and to paddle
:::
:::
```

Each `:::frame{slot="branch"}` is one branch. Copy one and the page has four boxes and four lines; take one out and it has two. The connector names `branch[*]`, every branch the page has, so the lines follow the Markdown without a change to the module.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/tree.webp" alt="The Design panel with the module Tree of ideas open at its connector card, and the review page with a box on top joined by lines with arrows to three boxes below." caption="The tree: one root, a grid that repeats the branch, and one connector to every branch." />

An org chart is the same module with other frames: a card for the head of the team, a grid that repeats a photo and a name, and a tree connector to every card. A timeline sets **Bar position** to 0: no bar, a drop from the strip of dates to each card.

## 9. Notes on a map, with pointers and a key

A page of notes round a map: each note has a line to the place it speaks of, the map has a key to its signs, and one name stands away from its point with a line back to it. None of these lines is part of the painting.

The book has the painting twice: `mapa`, with the names of pages 6 and 7, and `rio`, the same picture without anchors, set small on the contents page. This page uses `rio`, so the points you add to it do not move a name of the spread.

1. Make a page template as in section 2. **ID** `map-notes`, **Name** `Map with notes`. Its frames: `title` (**Box style** `portada`, **Takes** *Level 1*), `text` (**Takes the page's loose text**), `map` (**Width** *Up to the edge*), and `note-1`, `note-2` and `note-3` (**Box style** `dato`, **Width** 56 mm, **Smallest height** 26 mm). Stack `title`, `text` and `map` in a group `column` with 6 mm between frames, and lay the notes in a group `notes` *In a row* with 8 mm between them. Hang `note-1` from *Frame map*, *Below*, 14 mm down.
2. Write the page at the end of the chapter:

```md
::::page{template="map-notes"}
# Along the river

The map shows the valley of the Aldara. Each note tells you about one place on it: follow its line to find the place.

:::figure{resource="rio" slot="map"}
::label[Spring]{at="spring" style="agua"}
:::label{at="key" style="key"}
:icon[]{glyph="●" color="ink"} Town
:icon[]{line="solid" color="agua"} River
:icon[]{line="dashed" color="ink"} Path
:::
:::

:::frame{slot="note-1" label="The dam"}
The dam holds the water back in a lake. In dry months it lets some out, so the river never runs empty.
:::

:::frame{slot="note-2" label="Molinar"}
Molinar grew round its bridge and its mill. The mill wheel turned with the water of the river.
:::

:::frame{slot="note-3" label="Aldamar"}
At Aldamar the river meets the sea. Fishing boats leave the harbour every morning.
:::
::::
```

`:::label{at="key" style="key"}` is a label of several lines: each line is a row of the key. `:icon[…]{…}` is a symbol on the line: a glyph, a sample of a line, or a picture of the book. The **Symbol for a label** button of the Text panel's toolbar writes one at the caret, with its colour and size; the brackets hold what a screen reader says for it, and empty brackets mean the words beside it say it all.
3. Give the key its look. In **Design › Figures & tables › Label styles** press **Add label style**, set its id to `key`, its face to Fredoka at 10.5 pt, a white **Plate** with a **Plate padding** of 2.5 mm, an **Outline** and a **Corner radius** of 1.5 mm, and under **Lines and key** switch **Key (legend)** on. The symbols then stand in a column of their own and the words of every row start on one edge.
4. Place the points. **Checks** lists *Label at an unknown anchor* for `spring` and `key`. Press the pin button of one: the Anchors dialog of `rio` opens with both names under *Labels without an anchor*. Click the waterfall at the top left of the painting for `spring`, and the lower left corner for `key`; set the key's **Alignment** to *Start* and its **Vertical alignment** to *Bottom*, so it grows up and to the right of its point. Then click the dam, the mill and the harbour, and name the three new anchors `dam`, `mill` and `harbour` in their rows.
5. Set the name of the spring away from its point. Pick `spring` and, under **Label and leader**, switch **Away from its point** on. Drag the label's handle up into the sky and set **Leader** to *On*: the line from the label to the waterfall is drawn as you drag. Press **Save**.
6. Draw the pointers. In **Design** mode, pick `note-1` on the page: a round grip shows beside the middle of its right side. Drag it onto the dam; the anchors of the map show while you drag, and the drop writes a connector from the note to the anchor `dam` with a dot at its end. Do the same from `note-2` to `mill` and from `note-3` to `harbour`. The frame's **Pointers** group lists them, and is the way to add or change one with the keyboard.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/map-notes.webp" alt="The page Along the river in Design mode: the map with a key in its lower left corner, three notes under it with a line from each to a place on the map, and the Pointers group of the second note in the Design panel." caption="Each note points at an anchor of the picture; the key and the names are lines of the Markdown." />

A pointer ends at the anchor's point of the picture, through its crop and scale, so it still finds the mill when the map is set smaller or cut to its safe area. It is painted over the frames, so it shows over the picture. A note placed on top of the map instead, with no room of its own, is listed by Checks as *Frame under another frame*, with the fixes (see [Sandbox: composed pages](/en/docs/sandbox-composed-pages#overlaps-and-glyph-tabs)).

## 10. A page of photo cards

A page of cards (photos with a caption under each, portraits with a name) is one card drawn many times. A **grid that repeats a frame set** draws the card once, and the page has as many cards as its Markdown writes.

1. Make a template `photos` (**Name** `Photo cards`) with `title` (`portada`, *Level 1*) and `text` (the loose text) stacked in a group `column`, 6 mm apart.
2. Add a frame `photo`: **Box style** `foto`, hung from *Frame text*, *Below*, 6 mm down. Add a frame `caption`: **Box style** `dato`, **Width** *Its content*, hung from *Frame photo*, *Below*, 2 mm down.
3. Add a group `cards`: **Grid**, **Columns** 3, **Gap between columns** 6 mm, **Gap between rows** 12 mm. Under **Repeat a set of frames**, **Main frame** `photo`, and tick `caption` under **Frames that go with it**. The main frame takes the width of a cell; the caption hangs from it as the template draws it.
4. Write the page:

```md
::::page{template="photos"}
# People and the river

People use the river in many ways. Look at the photos and say what each person is doing.

:::frame{slot="photo"}
::resource{id="riego"}

:::frame{slot="caption"}
Watering the fields
:::
:::

:::frame{slot="photo"}
::resource{id="molino"}

:::frame{slot="caption"}
Grinding wheat
:::
:::

:::frame{slot="photo"}
::resource{id="piraguas"}

:::frame{slot="caption"}
Paddling a canoe
:::
:::

:::frame{slot="photo"}
::resource{id="limpieza"}

:::frame{slot="caption"}
Cleaning the banks
:::
:::
::::
```

Each `:::frame{slot="photo"}` is one card, and the `:::frame{slot="caption"}` inside it goes to the caption of that card. The cards fill the rows from the side where a line starts, so the fourth opens a second row. In Design mode the frames are named by their card: `photo[2]` and `caption[2]` are the second card.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/grid.webp" alt="The page People and the river with four photos in a grid of three columns and a caption under each, and the Design panel showing the group cards with Grid, three columns and Repeat a set of frames." caption="The card is drawn once; the Markdown gives four, and the grid lays four." />

A fifth photo takes the next cell; a seventh starts a third row. An edition in another language can have more cards or fewer with the same template. **Most items** caps the grid (what is past it goes to the loose text, and Checks lists *More items than the grid holds*), and **Content that does not fit goes to** *A page added after this one* on the main frame carries the rows that do not fit to an added page, whole.

## 11. A module library for a series

**Page & columns › Modules** is the book's module library. Each module is drawn with its frames and named by its id, with how many blocks of the Markdown use it on how many pages (*1 block on 1 page* for the tree), a pencil that opens it in the template editor, and the button that inserts it into the page chosen in **Page to insert into**. **New module** starts another. Renaming a module rewrites every `:::module` block that names it.

<Screenshot src="/docs-shots/en/modules.webp" alt="The Modules section of the Design panel: the list Page to insert into, the module Tree of ideas with the words 1 block on 1 page, and the New module button." caption="The library: each module, where the book uses it, and the way to insert it into a page." />

A page takes modules in a group with **Takes the page's modules** on, one under another in the order of the Markdown. So the next page can hold a tree under a box, or two trees, with no new template: the Markdown says which blocks and in what order, the modules draw them.

Modules can also stand side by side. Set the modules group's **Frames** to **In a row** or **Grid**, or add a second group with **Takes the page's modules** on, choose the first one in **Inside the group** and tick its **Modules it takes**: each run of those modules in the Markdown becomes one row of the stack. A module's **Width in its group**, set in its editor, shows under its name in the library.

The books of a series (*Our river* for Year 3, *Our coast* for Year 4) share their modules by sharing the design:

- **In the Sandbox.** Start the next book as a copy of this one (**Books**, **⋯** on the book, **Duplicate**) and write its chapters; the templates, the modules and the styles come with the copy. For a book that exists already, **Export** in the header of the Design panel saves the design, and **Import** in the other book puts it in place of that book's design.
- **From code.** Keep the module templates in one file and add them to the `pageTemplates` of every book of the series:

```ts
// series/modules.js: the tree and the other modules of the series
export const seriesModules = [
  { id: 'tree', name: 'Tree of ideas', role: 'module', frames: [/* root, branch */], groups: [/* branches */],
    decoration: { elements: [{ kind: 'connector', id: 'connector-1', routing: 'tree', from: { to: '#root' }, to: [{ to: '#branch[*]' }] }] } },
];

// our-coast/config.js
import { seriesModules } from '../series/modules.js';
export const config = { /* … */ pageTemplates: [...seriesModules, ...pagesOfThisBook] };
```

A change to a module (a thicker line, another box style) then reaches every page of every book that uses it the next time the book is laid out, and the words stay in each book's Markdown.

## What the Markdown says and what the design draws

The pages of sections 8 to 11 follow one rule. The Markdown says **what** goes on the page and **how many**: the words of each box, how many branches, notes, cards and rows of the key, which module comes first. The design draws each thing **once**: the boxes, their places, the lines between them, the look of the key, the size of a cell. That is why a translation needs no template of its own, why a page with five photos uses the template of a page with four, and why a series can share its modules. A template never holds the words of a page, and the Markdown never holds a position or a colour.
