## Introduction

The aim of **biochemistry** is to explain the structures and the functions of living things in chemical terms. Understanding the chemistry of biomolecules is the first step towards knowing what structure they have, how they interact and therefore what their biological function is.

This chapter confines itself to describing the fundamental concepts of organic chemistry needed to understand the characteristics of biochemical compounds and their reactivity. **Organic chemistry** is usually taken to be the chemistry of carbon and its compounds, although it also covers some simple inorganic compounds such as the oxides, the carbides and the carbonates. It is important to bear in mind that the chemistry of carbon is the basis of the chemistry of living things. One has to know the chemical elements that living things are made of, understand all the parameters life needs in order to develop, the need for water and for oxygen, and the thermodynamic characteristics that define a biological system.

## Chemical foundations

### Matter is made of atoms

The fundamental unit of matter is the **atom**, a particle of very small size (of the order of 10^−8^ cm), itself made up of subparticles: the **proton**, the **neutron** and the **electron**, whose charges and masses are given in :ref{id="tabla-1-1" style="full" case="lower"}.

::resource{id="tabla-1-1"}

Under normal conditions atoms carry no net charge: they have as many protons as electrons. There are, however, charged atoms, called **ions**, with a difference in charge. Atoms that lose electrons have more protons than electrons, their charge is positive and they form **cations**; atoms that gain electrons have more negative charges and make up **anions**.

The protons and the neutrons sit in the nucleus of the atom, where almost all its mass is concentrated. The electrons are found around it, in the atomic orbitals described further on.

Each **chemical element** is made of one kind of atom, told apart by the number of protons in its nucleus; this **atomic number (Z)** defines every element (:ref{id="fig-1-1"}). One and the same element may, however, vary in its number of neutrons, which gives rise to the **isotopes**: different atomic forms of a single element that differ in mass.

:::callout{type="key-concepts" title="KEY CONCEPTS"}
- **Atomic number (Z):** the number of protons.

- **Mass number (A):** the mass of the atom relative to that of hydrogen. It is the sum of the neutrons and the protons. It used to be called the atomic weight.

- **Isotope:** an atom of the same element with a different number of neutrons in its nucleus. Every isotope of an element has the same Z. Isotopes may be stable or radioactive.

- **Atom:** from the Latin *atomus*, and that from the Greek *α*´*τομος*: indivisible.
:::

### The atomic orbitals are defined by the quantum numbers

:::paragraphs{style="compact"}
Electrons are found in **atomic orbitals**, the regions around the nucleus where the probability of finding them is greatest. Every particular atom has a definite number of orbitals, each characterised by a given potential energy. Without going into the mathematics behind them, we can say that each orbital is defined by a set of three numbers, the **quantum numbers**:
:::

1. The first, known as the **principal quantum number** (written as the letter **n**), describes the size and the energy of the orbital. As its size grows, so do its energy and its distance from the nucleus. There are thus orbitals 1, 2, 3…

2. The second (written as the letter **l**) is known as the **azimuthal quantum number**. It represents an energy sublevel and defines the geometry of the orbital (spherical, lobed, and so on). These are written with the letters **s**, **p**, **d** and **f**.

3. A third number, the **magnetic quantum number** (**m**~l~), defines the orientation in space once arbitrary reference axes (**x**, **y**, **z**) are fixed. A p-type orbital, for example, may be p~x~, p~y~ or p~z~.

:::paragraphs{style="compact"}
These three numbers therefore define the atomic orbitals completely as to their energy, size, shape and orientation in space.
:::

The electrons are distributed among these orbitals according to several principles. First, electrons fill the lowest energy levels first. Second, each orbital holds at most two electrons. Last, where there are several possible positions in sublevels of the same energy, the electrons occupy separate sublevels, following the principle of maximum multiplicity. In the case of the p orbitals, for instance, three electrons would be placed one in each sublevel: p~x~^1^, p~y~^1^, p~z~^1^.

Given this distribution, a fourth number is needed to tell apart the two electrons of one orbital: the **spin number**, which reflects the motion of the electrons about an imaginary axis in a magnetic field.

Because atomic orbitals are hard to draw, a simplified approximation is used to represent the **electron configuration**: a set of boxes filled from the lowest energy level upwards according to how many electrons the element has (:ref{id="fig-1-2"}).

:::callout{type="key-concepts" title="KEY CONCEPTS"}
- **Atomic orbitals:** regions in space where the probability of finding electrons is greatest.

- **Energy levels:** electrons fill the levels from the lowest energy upwards. Each level may hold more than one orbital. Each orbital, defined by the three quantum numbers, may hold at most two electrons.

- Elements belonging to the same **group** have the same number of electrons in their outer shell (the valence shell).

- Elements belonging to the same **period** have the same energy level (n).
:::

### What determines the order of the elements in the periodic table?

:ref{id="fig-1-3" style="full" case="lower"} shows a periodic table in which only the chemical elements found in living things are marked. Each element's position in the table reveals its characteristics.

Every cell of the periodic table holds one element, identified by a symbol, its mass number and its atomic number. The order of the elements in the periodic table is set by two axes: a horizontal one (the periods) and a vertical one (the groups) (:ref{id="fig-1-4"}). Within a period the elements are ordered, from left to right, by their rising number of protons and therefore of electrons, if the atom is neutral. At the end of the period the last shell, or energy level, of that period will have been completed, and filling begins in the next one. The last element of each period has its last energy level complete and is called a **noble gas**.

### Elements combine and form molecules

An element is the more stable the closer it comes to an electron configuration in which its orbitals are complete. Save in exceptional cases, atoms tend to associate into molecules or atomic aggregates. Atoms join through **chemical bonds**, and these new polyatomic aggregates (**molecules**) behave as the elementary units of new substances. Sometimes two identical atoms associate (as in the oxygen molecule, O~2~). Molecules made up of atoms of different elements are called **compounds** (the water molecule, for example, formed by two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom: H~2~O).

Most chemical reactions come from the making and breaking of chemical bonds, so the nature of these bonds needs to be understood.

It is the electron configuration of each element that determines its **reactivity**. The electrons of the outer shells, which occupy the highest energy levels, are the ones that take part in chemical reactions, and they are known as the **valence electrons**.

The **octet rule** is useful for understanding how bonds form; it rests on the chemical behaviour of the **noble gases**. These elements have little tendency to react chemically because their electron configuration has a complete outer shell (the shell of the valence electrons, which holds eight electrons, except in helium, which holds two).

:::callout{type="key-concepts" title="KEY CONCEPTS"}
- The **valence** of a given chemical element can be defined as its combining capacity, and is a digit giving the number of bonds through which the element takes part in the compound. The valence of carbon, for example, is four, so it can form four bonds.

- **Electronegativity** can be defined as the tendency of atoms to draw the shared electron pair towards themselves.
:::

By this octet rule, put forward by Lewis, atoms are more stable when they reach eight electrons in the valence shell. This can be shown simply in Lewis notation, with the chemical symbol of each element surrounded by dots standing for the valence electrons (**Box 1-1**).

Before studying the bonds themselves, a property of great importance in explaining how they form and what they are like must be defined: **electronegativity**, the tendency of atoms to draw the shared electron pair towards themselves. The more electrons an atom has, the easier it is to complete its outer shell; atoms with more electrons in that shell are therefore more electronegative. Among the elements found in living things, oxygen and nitrogen are more electronegative than carbon and hydrogen, whose electronegativities are similar (see :ref{id="fig-1-4"}).

:::callout{type="recuadro" label="BOX 1-1" title="The octet rule"}
:::columns{count=2 breaks="3"}
Lewis's method explains some bonds simply; it holds that the elements lying close to the noble gases tend to take up, give up or share electrons until they reach the eight electrons that characterise the noble gases.

By this octet rule, atoms are more stable when they reach eight electrons in the valence shell — whether as lone or as shared pairs — through a covalent bond. In each single covalent bond, each atom of the pair contributes one electron; when drawing a Lewis diagram or structure, then, one must avoid assigning more than eight electrons to any atom.

There are exceptions, however. Hydrogen, for instance, has a single electron in its valence shell, which can accept at most two electrons; it can therefore share its electron with only one atom, forming a single bond. Non-metallic atoms from the third period onwards, on the other hand, may form "expanded octets" — that is, they may hold more than eight electrons in their valence shell, usually by placing the extra electrons in sublevels.
:::

::resource{id="recuadro-1-1-tabla-1"}
:::

When the reacting atoms are both highly electronegative, the bond forms because the two elements share their valence electrons until their outer shells are complete: this kind of bond, the **covalent bond** (:ref{id="fig-1-5"}), is the one mainly found in biological molecules. In the covalent bond there is no complete transfer of electrons, as there is in the ionic bond. From the combination of the two atomic orbitals a **molecular orbital** arises, and it determines the characteristics of the join. Molecular orbitals may have a lower energy than the atomic orbitals they came from, which stabilises the system and favours their formation; this kind of stable orbital is known as a **bonding orbital**. Or they may have a higher energy than the starting atomic orbitals, which destabilises the system; these are called **antibonding orbitals**, and they do not favour the formation of the bond.

The particular distribution of the electrons within a molecule is called its **electron configuration**. Under normal conditions this distribution has the lowest possible potential energy, and the molecule is then said to be in its **ground state**. There are other, higher-energy alternatives; in those the molecule has a greater potential energy and is said to be in an **excited state**.

#### Hybrid orbitals. The tetravalence of carbon

For the elements of the second period of the periodic table, among them carbon (C), nitrogen (N) and oxygen (O), the **s** and **p** orbitals of the outer shell lie so close in energy that they can interact and form **hybrid orbitals** combining the character of both. These hybrid orbitals let the element form as many bonds as possible while keeping them as far apart as possible, so as to minimise repulsive forces. The hybrid orbitals formed by carbon are the best studied, and they explain the nature of its bonds with other molecules (:ref{id="fig-1-6"}).

#### The coordinate, or dative, covalent bond

In the covalent bonds studied so far, each electron of the shared pair comes from one of the atoms taking part in the bond. In some cases, however, the shared pair comes from one atom alone, while the other contributes an empty orbital. The result is a positively charged molecule, the charge coming from the atom that contributes the electron-free orbital and therefore has the greater number of protons. For a bond of this kind to form, one atom must have a bond-free pair of electrons — a **lone pair** — in its outermost level (as oxygen and nitrogen do), and the other must have an empty orbital (as the proton, H^+^, does) (:ref{id="fig-1-7"}).

### Polarity and polar bonds

When two atoms of very different electronegativity form a covalent bond, the electrons are not shared equally by the two, and are drawn more strongly by the more electronegative one. A **polar covalent bond** then forms (:ref{id="fig-1-8"}), in which the more electronegative atom carries a greater density of negative charge (written δ^−^), while the other takes on a density of positive charge (written δ^+^), caused by the absence of the electron that used to balance the positive charge of the nucleus. The result is a **dipole**: two charges of opposite sign separated by a given distance. Bonds of this kind matter greatly for understanding the non-covalent interactions that may take place between different molecules. The water molecule has polar covalent bonds, and they are fundamental in explaining the solubility of the various biological molecules in water.

:::callout{type="key-concepts" title="KEY CONCEPTS"}
- The **functional groups** are the various associations between atoms that give molecules their functional characteristics.
:::

### The functional groups determine how biomolecules interact

The many ways the carbon atom has of forming different molecules follow from its capacity to form four bonds at very wide angles, bonds that are moreover covalent, non-polar and therefore very stable. Biological molecules can thus form long linear, branched and even cyclic structures, all of them very firm. Because the join between carbon and hydrogen is non-polar in nature, however, biological molecules — which live in a polar medium, water — need the help of other atoms that let them make and break bonds, and so make these molecules more reactive. A "living molecule", or biomolecule, must be in constant change; it will thus form very important associations, either among themselves or with water, since water is the medium they are mostly found in.

The chemical elements fundamental to the reactivity of biomolecules are O and N, both electronegative atoms, which make the molecules that carry them react with one another.

A series of **functional groups** recurs throughout the various biomolecules of living things. The nature of these groups decides how the biological molecule works, both for establishing covalent bonds between molecules and building biopolymers, or macromolecules, and for association and interaction through weak bonds with one another and with the medium.

### The weak interactions determine the function of the molecule

Every biological process happens thanks to the weak interactions established between molecules. Molecules must interact to begin an action and then come apart again. The unique binding and recognition between an enzyme and a substrate, or between a receptor and its ligand, as well as the replication and transcription of DNA, are processes that take place thanks to a particular orientation and binding between the molecules involved. These interactions are weak, but many of them together in the right position make the binding highly specific and strong, as well as vital to life in the cell.

Weak interactions may be electrostatic or hydrophobic in nature. The first kind takes in hydrogen bonds, salt bridges (the ionic bond) and Van der Waals forces.

#### The hydrogen bond

This kind of interaction is relatively strong in nature. It is very common between polar molecules in an aqueous medium and is responsible for the many weak joins between water molecules.

For a hydrogen bond to form there must be a hydrogen atom (H) covalently bound to an electronegative atom (usually O or N) which, because of its partial positive charge, is drawn by another electronegative atom in a different molecule (:ref{id="fig-1-9"}).

#### The ionic bond, or salt bridge

In the cell, the ions (Na^+^, K^+^ or Cl^−^, for example) establish electrostatic interactions with one another (between opposite charges), also called salt bridges (:ref{id="fig-1-10"}).

Moreover, those functional groups that behave as acids or bases — that is, that can give a proton to the medium or take one from it — carry a real charge (one electron more or fewer than the neutral atom should have), which turns them into an ion. Ions in aqueous solution may attract or repel one another according to the charge they carry. Electrostatic attraction of this kind would behave as an ionic bond; it is nevertheless considered a weak interaction, since an ion in aqueous solution is solvated (surrounded by water molecules), and that weakens the bond between oppositely charged ions.

Salt bridges do not depend on the distance or the orientation between atoms as strongly as hydrogen bonds do.

- Ions in aqueous solution establish electrostatic interactions, or **salt bridges**. Ionic interaction of this kind is weak, because of the **screening** the water molecules around the ion bring to the interaction.

- **Hydrophobic:** phobia, hatred of water.

- **Hydrophilic:** attraction to water.
:::

#### Van der Waals forces

These are very weak interactions that hold non-polar atoms or molecules together for a time. They too are charge-charge interactions, but they depend on the distance between atoms. They are "temporary dipoles", present only at the moment when the electron of one atom moves towards or away from the atom it is bound to. Temporary dipoles of this kind form continually between molecules in solution, and the molecules need not be polar: a temporary dipole may therefore arise in a non-polar covalent bond. The constant motion and redistribution of the electrons in the molecule produce complicated, fluctuating changes in its attraction or repulsion.

#### The hydrophobic interaction

Hydrophobic forces differ from those described above in that they are not electrostatic in nature. They therefore occur between non-polar molecules and functional groups. Nor is there any interaction between them at all: the join rests solely on the hydrophobic molecule's inability to interact with water. The force that holds non-polar, or hydrophobic, molecules together rests on their tendency to drive water out of their surroundings, because of their repulsion of (insolubility in) the polar groups of water (:ref{id="fig-1-11"}).

Hydrophobic interactions are fundamental in biology, since the non-polar nature of many components forces them to stay together, forming various structures, in order to get away from water and so build true hydrophobic barriers — such as the lipid membranes that define cells and their organelles.

## Water as the principal biological solvent

### The water molecule is a dipole

Water is the fundamental liquid medium in which most of the cell's chemical reactions take place. It is therefore the principal biological solvent. The water molecule has the chemical characteristic of behaving as a dipole: the O atom with a partial negative charge (δ^−^), and the two H atoms with a partial positive charge (δ^+^). This arrangement is due to the difference in electronegativity between the H atoms and the O atom (which is highly electronegative, since it is only two electrons short of completing its outer shell). The distribution of the charges and the geometry of the molecule make possible the strong interaction between one molecule and its neighbours. The weak interactions a water molecule establishes with those around it are hydrogen bonds (:ref{id="fig-1-12"}). Weak bonds of this kind are vitally important, not only because they let bonds form and break and so give water its liquid nature, but also because it is thanks to this kind of interaction that many biological molecules dissolve in this medium.

:::

The number of protons is therefore equal to the number of hydronium ions (**Box 1-4**).

### The chemistry of acids and bases

The behaviour of the ionisation of water is the basis for understanding the concepts of acid and base. The definition accepted today is Lewis's, by which a base is a substance with a pair of electrons available to form a dative covalent bond, while an acid is a molecule holding an atom able to accept a pair of electrons, since it has a free outer orbital.

In many cases, however, the older Bronsted-Lowry definition is useful: an acid is defined as a substance that can give up a proton and a base as one that can accept a proton on reacting with an acid. When the acid loses the proton it becomes a substance that tends to take it back, and for that reason this second form is called its **conjugate base**. In the same way, a base that takes up a proton will tend to lose it and will therefore be acidic in character. Some substances can behave both as acids and as bases; they are called **amphoteric** substances. Water is one of them and, in aqueous solutions, acts as an acid in the presence of a base or as a base in the presence of an acid.

Among biological molecules only a few functional groups behave as an acid or a base. Knowing how they behave in the physiological medium matters, given what it may imply for living things (:ref{id="fig-1-14"}).

### pH and pKa

The acidity of a solution is measured by the concentration of hydronium ions, or protons, it holds. This concentration runs from 1 molar (1 m) in a very acidic solution to 10^−14^ m in a very alkaline, or basic, one. To avoid using such small numbers, it was decided to convert these concentrations to a logarithmic scale (**Box 1-5**), called the **pH scale**, which runs from 0 to 14 (:ref{id="tabla-1-2" style="full"}).

:::callout{type="recuadro" label="BOX 1-5" title="Remember… the logarithm" span="column"}
A fraction can be written as a negative exponent:

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
$\frac{1}{10}$ = 10^−1^
:::

The **logarithm** is the mathematical function inverse to the exponential function.

The logarithm of a number *x* is the exponent (*n*) to which the given base (usually 10) must be raised to give that number *x*.

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
Log~10~ *x* = *n* ⇌ *x* = 10^n^
:::

Example:

Log~10~ 10 = 1 ⇌ *x* = 10^1^ = 10, *n* = 1−

Log 10^7^ = 7 ⇌ *x* = 10^7^, *n* = 7−

Log 10^−7^ = −7 ⇌ *x* = 10^−7^, *n* = −7

When the base is 10 the subscript is left out.
:::

:::paragraphs{style="compact"}
pH is defined as pH = −log~10~ [H^+^], where [H^+^] is the molar concentration: the number of moles of H^+^ per litre of solution.

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
[H~3~O^+^] = [OH^−^] = 1 × 10^−7^ mol/litre

pH = −log [H~3~O^+^] = −log [H^+^]

pH = −log [1 × 10^−7^]

pH = 7
:::

At 25 °C, the pH of pure water and of any aqueous solution holding equal concentrations of hydronium ion and hydroxyl ion is 7.

### Buffer solutions regulate the pH of the cell

Certain functional groups present in biological molecules can behave as weak acids or bases. Their state of ionisation will therefore depend on the proton concentration of the medium. Bearing in mind that most enzymes carry ionisable groups of this kind in their active site, it becomes clear how important a small fluctuation in cellular pH can be. If an amino group of a residue of an enzyme carries a positive charge at pH 7, for instance, a slight rise in pH may force the H^+^ of the amino group to be given up to the medium, so that the charge is lost. In many cases that charge is essential for the enzyme to interact with the substrate; the enzyme will then stop working. The far-reaching consequences of the ionisation of the groups of the amino acids present in proteins, and in enzymes in particular, are reviewed closely in chapters 6 and 8 respectively.

Regulating pH is therefore essential, both inside and outside the cell, if the molecules are to work at their best. **Buffers** are aqueous systems that tend to damp down the changes in pH that occur when small amounts of acid (H^+^) or base (OH^−^) are added. These buffer systems are made up of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or else of a weak base and its conjugate acid. When the concentrations of the two species are similar, the system has a great buffering capacity. In that situation any ­rise in the concentration of H^+^ can be absorbed by the conjugate base, and if the concentration of OH^−^ rises it is the weak acid of the buffer system that gives a proton to the medium to neutralise the hydroxyl ion. By the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation (**Box 1-6**), when the pH of the solution equals the p*K*~a~ of the system, the concentrations of the two species that define the system are equal. In the cell, then, substances with a p*K*~a~ near 7 (physiological pH) make good buffers.

:::callout{type="recuadro" label="BOX 1-6" title="Calculating the dissociation constant of an acid, and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation"}
:::columns{count=2 breaks="8"}
A general dissociation reaction of an acid may be written as the general reaction:

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
HA ⇌ A^−^ + H^+^
:::

This reaction is characterised by an equilibrium constant which, for an acid, is called ***K***~a~, or the **acid dissociation constant**, and is given by the formula:

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
*K*~a~ = $\frac{[A^{-}] [H^{+}]}{[HA]}$
:::

A strong acid such as HCl will have a high *K*~a~, since, being largely dissociated, the concentrations of the products of the reaction will be greater than those of the reactants.

Just as pH was defined as −log [H^+^], the degree of dissociation of an acid can be expressed as −log *K*~a~. This expression is called the p*K*~a~, and its value is inversely proportional to the strength of the acid.

The p*K*~a~ of weak acids can be worked out by running a **titration curve**, and what the value means can be seen by following the variation in the concentration of the different molecular forms at the various pH values, plotted on the graph.

::resource{id="recuadro-1-6-fig"}
:::

At low pH the protonated forms prevail (here CH~3~ ⇌ COOH, uncharged); they fall away as the pH rises, until a pH is reached at which the dominant form is the dissociated one (CH~3~ ⇌ COO^−^, negatively charged). Half way through the titration the solution passes through a state in which the concentrations of the two forms are the same ([CH~3~ ⇌ COOH] = [CH~3~ ⇌ COO^−^]).

pH and p*K*~a~ can be related through the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
pH = p*K*~a~ + log $\frac{[A^{-}]}{[HA]}$ $\frac{(proton acceptor)}{(proton donor)}$
:::

This relation shows that when the concentrations of A^−^ (proton acceptor) and HA (proton donor) are equal, p*K*~a~ = pH, since the log of 1 is zero.

Two conclusions follow from this relation:

1. The pH that matches the p*K*~a~ is the one at which the acid-conjugate base pair has its greatest buffering power, since the concentration of proton acceptor able to neutralise protons when an acid is added is the same as that of proton donor, able to neutralise the OH^−^ groups that rise when a base is added.

2. At a pH below the p*K*~a~ the protonated forms prevail, and above that value the deprotonated forms are in the majority; as a consequence, the net charge of the molecule changes.
:::

The main intracellular biological buffer is the phosphate buffer, whose p*K*~a~ is 6.86 and which can therefore resist changes of pH between 5.9 and 7.9.

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
H~2~PO~4~^−^ ⇌ H^+^ + HPO~4~^2−^
:::

The main buffer of the blood is the bicarbonate buffer system, made up of the weak acid carbonic acid and its conjugate base bicarbonate (**Box 1-7**).

:::callout{type="recuadro" label="BOX 1-7" title="Acidosis and alkalosis"}
:::columns{count=2 breaks="7"}
Acidosis or alkalosis arises when the production and the elimination of acids or bases in the body fall out of step and the buffering mechanisms are overwhelmed, so that blood pH leaves its physiological range, which lies between 7.35 and 7.45. Chief among the mechanisms that buffer physiological pH are the phosphate buffer, the bicarbonate buffer and the plasma and cellular proteins (albumin and haemoglobin above all).

The body has a great capacity to produce acid, but a more limited capacity to produce bases. The acids produced in the body are usually classed as:

- **Fixed acids.** They arise from the incomplete metabolism of fatty acids, sugars or amino acids, which yields acids such as lactic acid or the ketone bodies. Sulphates are also produced from the metabolism of the sulphur-bearing amino acids, and phosphates from the metabolism of the phospholipids and nucleotides.

- **Volatile acids.** This is the carbon dioxide that arises from aerobic metabolism during the complete breakdown of sugars and fatty acids in the Krebs cycle. Carbon dioxide reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen carbonate and protons.

The human body produces a great deal of acid. It is estimated to produce 40 mmol of fixed acids and 20,000 mmol of volatile acids a day. These compounds are eliminated by the kidney in the case of the fixed acids and by the lung in the case of the volatile ones.

The main buffering mechanism of blood plasma is the bicarbonate buffer, which can be controlled both in the lungs (the carbon dioxide) and in the kidney (hydrogen carbonate and protons), which makes this buffer an excellent mechanism for controlling changes in blood pH and, indirectly, in the whole organism. The equilibrium is set out below:

::resource{id="recuadro-1-7-fig"}

If the pH falls below 7.35 we speak of **acidosis**. If the cause has to do with the respiratory system (pCO~2~ > 45 mmHg) it is called **respiratory acidosis** — that produced by emphysema or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), for example. If the cause is a kidney problem or the excessive intake or production of acids ([HCO~3~^−^] < 21 mmol/L) it is called **metabolic acidosis**: excessive intake of acetylsalicylic acid, say, a lactic acidosis from a muscular problem, or a ketoacidosis from diabetes.

If the pH rises above 7.45 we speak of **alkalosis**. If the cause has to do with the respiratory system (pCO~2~ < 35 mmHg) it is called **respiratory alkalosis** — hyperventilation from an anxiety attack, for instance — whereas if the cause is a kidney problem or an excessive elimination of acids it is called **metabolic alkalosis** ([HCO~3~^−^] > 28 mmol/L): a loss of chlorides through ­vomiting, say, or a hyperaldosteronism affecting kidney function.

Disorders of pH are potentially very serious, since they markedly affect the working of proteins and the activity of enzymes. They also affect muscle contraction and nerve impulses, as a consequence of the disturbance of membrane potentials, partly through exchanges between protons and potassium. These disorders may bring on headaches, confusion, tachycardia, arrhythmias, a rise in blood pressure, fasciculations and, in severe cases, seizures and coma (putting the patient's life at risk).

## Chemical reactions in the cell

### The equilibrium of a chemical reaction

In a chemical reaction the reactants interact to form products. In most chemical reactions, however, the reaction does not end when all the reactant has turned into product. Instead, a moment comes when the product formed begins to react to give back reactant. That is, the reaction goes both ways. When the rate of the forward reaction (forming product) and the rate of the backward reaction (forming reactant) become equal, the reaction is said to have reached **equilibrium**. The great majority of the reactions that take place in the cell are **reversible reactions** and therefore tend towards this chemical equilibrium.

:::callout{type="question" title="REFLECTION QUESTION"}
What would the pH be of a solution in which the concentration of CH~3~COOH^–^ equals that of CH~3~COO^–^? Which species would be at the higher concentration if 0.5 equivalents of (OH^–^) were added? Using the graph, give the pH that corresponds to this solution.
:::

The rate of a reaction is set by a rate constant (*k*) and by the concentration of reactant. With *k*~1~ the rate constant of the forward reaction and *k*~−1~ the rate constant of the backward reaction,

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
A $\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}$ B
:::

:::paragraphs{style="compact"}
The rate of formation of B: *v*~B~ = *k*~1~ [A]

The rate of formation of A: *v*~A~ = *k*~−1~ [B]

At equilibrium: *v*~A~ = *v*~B~ ⟹ *k*~1~ [A] = *k*~−1~ [B]

Hence,
:::

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
$\frac{k_{1}}{k_{-1}}$ = $\frac{[B]}{[A]}$ = equilibrium *K*
:::

:::paragraphs{style="compact"}
At equilibrium the rates become equal and the ratio between the concentrations of reactants and products is constant.
:::

The principles of thermodynamics help to predict whether a chemical reaction happens spontaneously or not. If the reaction is spontaneous it is said to be far from equilibrium, so it will tend to form product spontaneously until it reaches equilibrium. In that case, at the start of the reaction and at constant temperature and pressure, the energy of the product will be much lower than that of the reactant, and the change of energy over the reaction will release energy from the system (the reaction) to the surroundings (the medium the reaction runs in), giving a value below zero. The change, or variation, in Gibbs free energy (Δ*G*) of a reaction is worked out as an increment:

:::callout{type="key-concepts" title="KEY CONCEPTS"}
- At **equilibrium** the ratio of the concentrations of products and reactants is constant (**K**~eq~), but that does not mean those concentrations are equal.
:::

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
Δ*G* = *G*~final~ − *G*~initial~
:::

If the reaction is at equilibrium it will not move, there will be no change, the reaction will have the same energy in the initial state as in the final one, and its Δ*G* will therefore be zero.

If the reaction has a positive Δ*G*, the reactant will have a lower energy than the product and the reaction cannot happen spontaneously. Energy from another reaction must then be used, coupled to the first, provided the sum of the two coupled reactions is below zero.

The variable *G* is a "state function", so its value does not depend on the path taken from the initial to the final state. For this reason a non-spontaneous reaction can be made spontaneous by coupling it to another that is favourable, thanks to a common intermediate.

The value of Δ*G* tells us about the spontaneity of the reaction, but says nothing about its rate. These concepts are taken further in chapter 10.

### The reactivity of biological molecules

The presence of functional groups in biomolecules provides ­reactive sites, where those molecules bind to others or react and are transformed.

Reactive sites may be nucleophilic or electrophilic, according to whether or not they attract electrons. The functional groups of molecules provide enzymes with the "sites of attack" where the enzyme turns a substrate into a product. Chapter 8 classifies enzymes into six categories according to the kind of reaction they catalyse.

The great number and variety of chemical reactions that take place inside the cell (the metabolic pathways) involve only a few reactive sites, which invariably call on the functional groups already described. These groups behave differently from the rest of the molecule they belong to. The importance of the functional groups for the "interaction" between macromolecules has already been described; what follows sets out how decisively they are involved in transforming those biomolecules to build the biological macromolecules.

Reactive sites carry nucleophilic or electrophilic centres:

**Nucleophilic centres** (attraction to the nucleus): these are electron-rich groups and may carry a negative charge, non-bonding electron pairs or "lone pairs", or the electron density typical of double bonds. These sites attack positively charged groups, to which they are drawn.

- **Electrophilic centres** (attraction to electrons): they are drawn to negative charges — that is, to electron-rich ones — because they lack electrons in the valence shell.

Note that a functional group may hold an electronegative atom that generates a dipole, so that it carries both a nucleophilic and an electrophilic centre (:ref{id="fig-1-15"}). These reactive centres are the basis of the various kinds of chemical reaction.

**Condensation**, or **dehydration**, **reactions** are a kind of chemical reaction that plays a fundamental part in building macromolecules. Various polar functional groups take part in reactions of this kind, and the formation of a covalent bond releases a molecule of water into the medium. Since biochemical reactions always take place in an aqueous medium (the cytoplasm or the extracellular medium), reactions of this kind are decisive in building polymers — that is, the macromolecules such as polysaccharides, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids studied throughout the book (:ref{id="fig-1-16"}).

**Box 1-8** sets out the different kinds of covalent condensation bond established between the various functional groups, which give rise to the great variety of biological molecules.

Another characteristic kind of reaction that takes place in the cell is the one in which electrons are transferred from one substrate to another: the **oxidation-reduction, or redox**, reactions. Most of the reactions that take place during cellular metabolism involve this transfer of electrons. Two reactants are always needed for it, one that gives electrons and another that accepts them. In biological molecules this transfer of electrons is usually carried out in the form of hydrogen atoms, since a hydrogen atom holds one electron (H = 1 *e*^−^)

:::paragraphs{style="equation"}
AH~2~ + B ⇌ A + BH~2~
:::

:::paragraphs{style="compact"}
where: AH~2~ = electron donor (reducing agent, reduced molecule) B = electron acceptor (oxidising agent, oxidised molecule)
:::

In biomolecules the redox processes of electron transfer take place at the carbon atoms. The most reduced form of a carbon atom is the one saturated with four hydrogens, as in methane, while the most oxidised is carbon dioxide.

The oxidation levels of carbon can easily be assessed by counting the number of bonds the carbon makes with hydrogen (the most reduced state) or with oxygen (the most oxidised state) (:ref{id="fig-1-17"}).

The energetic power of organic substances is the greater the greater their oxidising power (that is, the more reduced the substance), so a great deal of energy is released during oxidation. Cellular metabolism is what transforms and stores this energy content of the reduced molecules the cell uses as an energy source. Section II, on metabolism, examines these oxidation-reduction processes in detail.

## The cellular context

The levels of molecular organisation of the cell show that, at the first, molecular level, the cellular components are the monomers, or building blocks, the cell is built from. These monomers associate into polymers through condensation reactions, to give the second, macromolecular level (:ref{id="fig-1-18"}). The association between different macromolecules forms the supramolecular structures or complexes of the third level, above all through weak interactions. And the last level in the hierarchy is the cellular level, or the cellular organelles that mark out the spaces where the various reactions take place.

The cell is therefore the container. In a eukaryotic cell the cellular compartments are clearly distinct and their functions well specialised, so not every chemical reaction happens just anywhere in the cell. This specialisation into organelles can be carried out very efficiently because each organelle is bounded by a cell membrane whose chemical characteristics allow an arrangement in the form of a lipid bilayer, which separates two hydrophilic media. Chapter 10 gives a schematic view of the functions of each organelle and the processes carried out in it.

Lastly, a multicellular organism calls for a look at a level above the cellular one, and that level gives an organic view of how biochemical reactions run in the complete organism. Although the biochemical processes that take place in the cell are in many cases identical in cells of different species, and throughout the book we have sought out the points that unify the metabolism of eukaryotic cells, for certain processes one must attend to the system or organ the process runs in, since organic specialisation is fundamental to understanding how the organism works metabolically. Lipid synthesis, for example, is not carried out in just any cell: the liver is a primary organ, and it holds the enzymes needed to synthesise a great deal of these biomolecules. Although the specialisation of the various organs goes beyond the scope of this book, those metabolic processes that happen in a particular place in the cell are pointed out.
